# 13. Emergencies: what to do first

This chapter is about the first minutes: the number to call, the moves that save a life before paramedics arrive, and the alerts and kits that buy you time in a disaster. Items are ordered by value — cheapest and highest-benefit first. "Evidence A" means the claim rests on strong human studies; "Evidence B" means solid but thinner evidence, including official guidance stated as fact. Figures checked 2026-10-01.

**Canada-wide with provincial differences. Dialling 911, Alert Ready wireless alerts, and Environment Canada weather alerts work the same in every province and territory. Poison control numbers, health lines, and wildfire reporting lines are provincial — Quebec's numbers are printed separately below. Carbon monoxide alarm and fire code rules are provincial; the Ontario rule is given as an example and other provinces have their own codes.**

### 1. Call 911 for police, fire, and ambulance — one number covers everything
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a short call.
- In plain words: In Canada one emergency number reaches police, fire, and ambulance dispatch: 911. Call only when you need help right now — a fire, a crime in progress, a person badly hurt and unable to wait. Stay on the line until the dispatcher tells you to hang up. If you dial by mistake, do not hang up; tell the dispatcher it was an accident. Texting 911 does not work in most places.
- Benefit: One number — 911 — reaches police, fire, and ambulance dispatchers who send help at once; calling early shortens response time when minutes decide survival.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: RCMP — tips for calling emergency services — https://www.rcmp.ca/en/bc/safety-tips/emergency-services/tips-calling-emergency-services
- Notes: Government program rule stated as fact; graded B per the adaptation contract.

### 2. Call the poison centre first in any suspected poisoning
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a phone call.
- In plain words: For a swallowed poison, an overdose, a chemical splash in the eyes, and a bite from an unknown creature, call the poison centre before trying home remedies. Do not make the person vomit unless the specialist tells you to. Keep the container and the label nearby so you can read the exact name to the specialist.
- Benefit: Canada's poison line is 1-844-764-7669 (1-844-POISON-X) and Quebec's centre is 1-800-463-5060, both open 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Poisonings cause more than 1,500 deaths and about 8,000 hospital stays in Canada each year, so one early call is the highest-value first step.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Health Canada — message from the Minister of Health, Poison Prevention Week 2024 — https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/news/2024/03/message-from-the-minister-of-health-poison-prevention-week.html

### 3. Start chest compressions at once when an adult collapses and is not breathing normally
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; your hands and your nerve.
- In plain words: If an adult collapses, is unresponsive, and is not breathing normally, call 911 and start chest compressions immediately. Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, about two inches deep, at about one hundred to one hundred twenty pushes per minute. If you are untrained, skip rescue breaths — compressions alone keep blood moving to the brain. Keep going until paramedics arrive and take over.
- Benefit: Bystander compressions started before paramedics arrive are linked to roughly double the chance of surviving an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest; calling 911 first makes sure advanced help is already rolling.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Sasson C et al. (2010). Predictors of survival from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes. https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCOUTCOMES.109.889576

### 4. Act FAST on stroke signs and call 911 at once
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a fast call.
- In plain words: Face drooping, arm weakness, speech trouble — time to call 911. When one side of the face droops, one raised arm drifts down, and speech sounds slurred and strange, a stroke may be happening. Note the time the signs started and tell the dispatcher. Do not drive the person yourself, and do not give food, drink, and aspirin.
- Benefit: Clot-busting drugs and clot removal work only inside a short window after symptoms start, and every minute of delay kills more brain; calling 911 immediately gets the patient to a stroke-ready hospital inside that window.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Emberson J et al. (2014). Effect of treatment delay on stroke thrombolysis. The Lancet. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(14)60584-5

### 5. Call 911 for chest pain that feels new and heavy
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a fast call.
- In plain words: New, heavy pressure in the chest — like a weight sitting on it — lasting more than a few minutes needs 911 now. The pain can spread to the arm, neck, jaw, and back, and can come with shortness of breath, cold sweat, and nausea. Women and people with diabetes sometimes feel only breathlessness and unusual fatigue. Chew nothing and drive nowhere; wait for the ambulance.
- Benefit: Opening a blocked heart artery quickly limits heart muscle death; calling 911 instead of driving yourself means treatment starts in the ambulance and the hospital team is ready on arrival.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Karkabi M et al. (2021). Door-to-balloon time and outcomes. European Heart Journal Quality of Care and Clinical Outcomes. https://doi.org/10.1093/ehjqcco/qcaa037

### 6. Use back blows and abdominal thrusts for choking
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; your hands.
- In plain words: When a choking adult cannot breathe, cannot speak, and cannot cough, act now. Lean the person forward and give five sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand. If nothing comes out, stand behind the person, wrap your arms around the waist, make a fist above the belly button, and pull sharply inward and upward five times. Repeat the two sets until the object flies out and the person breathes again.
- Benefit: Alternating five back blows with five abdominal thrusts clears most airway blockages within seconds; doing nothing while waiting for 911 risks brain injury within minutes.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: MedlinePlus — choking first aid — https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000047.htm

### 7. Press hard on heavy bleeding; add a tourniquet high on the limb when pressure fails
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a cloth and firm hands.
- In plain words: For heavy bleeding, press the wound hard with a cloth and the palm of your hand, and keep the pressure on. When blood soaks through, add more cloth on top and press harder — do not peel off the first layer. When a limb wound keeps spurting despite firm pressure, wrap a tourniquet two to three inches above the wound, tighten until the bleeding stops, and note the time. Call 911 and keep the tourniquet on until medics take over.
- Benefit: Firm direct pressure stops most bleeding; a properly tightened tourniquet, placed before shock sets in and left until 911 crews arrive, sharply raises survival after severe limb bleeding.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Teixeira PGR et al. (2018). Tourniquet use in civilian trauma. Journal of the American College of Surgeons. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jamcollsurg.2018.01.047; Kragh JF et al. (2009). Survival with emergency tourniquet use. Annals of Surgery. https://doi.org/10.1097/SLA.0b013e31818842ba

### 8. Use the epinephrine auto-injector at the first sign of a severe allergic reaction
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: The price of the device, replaced before expiry.
- In plain words: Hives spreading fast, lips and tongue swelling, throat tightening, wheezing, and dizziness after a sting, a food, and a drug mean anaphylaxis — use the auto-injector now. Jab it into the outer thigh through clothing, hold for a count of ten, then call 911. A second dose can follow after five to fifteen minutes when symptoms keep worsening.
- Benefit: Epinephrine in the thigh within minutes of anaphylaxis onset reverses airway swelling and collapsing blood pressure; calling 911 at the same time brings the hospital care that prevents a rebound reaction hours later.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: EAACI (2022). Guidelines on anaphylaxis. Allergy. https://doi.org/10.1111/all.15032

### 9. Cool a burn under cool running water for twenty minutes
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; twenty minutes at the tap.
- In plain words: Hold a fresh burn under cool running water for twenty minutes — a full twenty by the clock. Take off rings and tight clothing near the burn before swelling starts, unless clothing is stuck to the skin. Do not use ice, butter, toothpaste, and egg white. Cover loosely with cling film and a clean cloth after cooling, and seek care for burns larger than the palm of the hand.
- Benefit: Twenty minutes of cool running water limits burn depth and lowers the chance of needing a skin graft; it is the single most effective first aid for a fresh thermal burn.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Griffin B et al. (2022). Cool running water and burn outcomes. Australasian Emergency Care. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.auec.2022.05.004

### 10. Treat sudden one-eye vision loss as a same-day emergency
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: An emergency department visit.
- In plain words: When one eye suddenly goes dark like a curtain dropping while the other eye sees fine, get to an emergency department the same day. The same applies to a sudden shower of floaters with flashing lights and a dark shadow spreading across the vision. Do not wait for morning, and do not book a routine eye appointment.
- Benefit: A painless curtain over one eye can signal a blocked retinal artery — the eye's version of a stroke — where treatment within hours can save sight.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Mac Grory B et al. (2021). Management of central retinal artery occlusion. Stroke. https://doi.org/10.1161/STR.0000000000000366; Rothwell PM et al. (2007). Effect of urgent treatment of transient ischaemic attack (EXPRESS study). The Lancet. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(07)61448-2

### 11. Rush to emergency for sudden eye pain with halos and nausea
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: An emergency department visit.
- In plain words: Sudden severe eye pain with headache, rainbow halos around lights, blurred vision, and nausea with vomiting can mean acute angle closure — pressure spiking inside the eye. This is an emergency department visit now, not tomorrow. Do not use leftover eye drops, and do not try to sleep it off.
- Benefit: Eye pressure in acute angle closure can destroy vision within hours; emergency treatment that lowers the pressure the same day preserves sight.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Zhou Y et al. (2022). Acute primary angle closure. Frontiers in Medicine. https://doi.org/10.3389/fmed.2022.1078237; Sung KR et al. (2023). Angle closure management. Clinical and Experimental Ophthalmology. https://doi.org/10.1111/ceo.14206

### 12. Call 911 for a thunderclap headache — the worst headache of your life
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a fast call.
- In plain words: A headache that hits like a thunderclap — peak pain within seconds, the worst headache of your life — needs a 911 call and a same-day head scan. The same urgency applies when the worst headache comes with fainting, a seizure, and a stiff neck with fever. Do not take painkillers and lie down hoping it passes.
- Benefit: A thunderclap headache can signal bleeding around the brain; a CT scan within hours of calling 911 finds the bleed while it is still treatable.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Perry JJ et al. (2013). Clinical decision rules for subarachnoid hemorrhage. JAMA. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2013.278018

### 13. Call 911 for tearing chest pain that moves to the back
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a fast call.
- In plain words: Sudden severe chest pain that feels like tearing and ripping, moving into the back and down between the shoulder blades, needs 911 at once. Fainting, a weak pulse on one side, and sudden trouble speaking alongside the pain make it more urgent. Stay still and wait for the ambulance; do not drive.
- Benefit: Tearing pain that migrates to the back can signal an aortic dissection, where the aorta's inner wall splits; reaching a surgical centre quickly after calling 911 is the difference between repair and rupture.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Hagan PG et al. (2000). The International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection (IRAD). JAMA. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.283.7.897

### 14. Get a same-day scan for a swollen painful leg followed by sudden breathlessness
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: An emergency department visit.
- In plain words: A leg that swells, turns red and warm, and aches — especially the calf after a long flight and a long car ride — can hide a deep vein clot. When sudden shortness of breath, sharp chest pain when breathing in, and coughing blood follow, the clot may have moved to the lungs. Go to emergency the same day; do not massage the leg.
- Benefit: A leg clot that travels to the lungs can kill within hours; same-day ultrasound and blood thinners stop the clot from moving further.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Goldhaber SZ et al. (1999). Acute pulmonary embolism (ICOPER). The Lancet. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(98)07534-5

### 15. After a head bump in an older adult, watch for slow changes and get a scan
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: An emergency department visit.
- In plain words: An older adult who bumps the head — even lightly, with no cut and no loss of consciousness — can bleed slowly inside the skull over days and weeks. Watch for a headache that grows, new confusion, unusual sleepiness, weakness on one side, and repeated vomiting. Any of these means an emergency visit and a head scan the same day.
- Benefit: Slow bleeding under the skull after a minor bump is common past retirement age and is curable when a scan finds it early; waiting until coma sets in makes surgery far riskier.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Dziho et al. (2025). Chronic subdural hematoma in the elderly. Brain and Spine. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bas.2025.105893; Mathon et al. (2026). Subdural hematoma management. Pratique Neurologique. https://doi.org/10.1684/pnv.2026.1274

### 16. Install carbon monoxide alarms on every storey and act on the alarm
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: One alarm per floor; a few minutes to mount and test.
- In plain words: Carbon monoxide has no smell and no colour; a working alarm is the only warning you get. In Ontario, a home needs CO alarms if it has a fuel-burning appliance. A fireplace alone also triggers the rule, and so does an attached garage — any single one of the three is enough. Since January 1, 2026, Ontario requires an alarm beside each sleeping area AND on every storey of the home, including floors with no bedrooms. When the alarm sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air at once and call 911 from outside. Do not open windows first, and do not go back inside for pets and belongings.
- Benefit: Ontario's rule triggers on any single one of fuel-burning appliance, fireplace, or attached garage — not all three at once. Since January 1, 2026, the Ontario Fire Code requires alarms on every storey, not just beside sleeping areas; when the alarm sounds, leaving immediately and calling 911 from outside avoids the confusion-to-collapse sequence of CO poisoning.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Hawkins-Gignac Act (Carbon Monoxide Safety), 2013, S.O. 2013, c. 14 — https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/s13014; Ontario — carbon monoxide safety (2026 Fire Code rules) — http://www.ontario.ca/page/carbon-monoxide-safety; U.S. CPSC — carbon monoxide information center — https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Carbon-Monoxide-Information-Center

### 17. During a seizure, protect from injury and time it — never put anything in the mouth
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; calm hands and a phone timer.
- In plain words: When someone seizes, ease the person to the floor, move hard and sharp objects away, and place something soft under the head. Turn the person onto the side once the shaking slows so fluids drain out. Time the seizure with your phone. Never put fingers, spoons, and wallets in the mouth — the person will not swallow the tongue. Call 911 when the seizure lasts past five minutes, when another follows at once, and when the person stays unconscious after it ends.
- Benefit: Most seizures end on their own within a couple of minutes and need no drugs; the side position prevents choking, and calling 911 for seizures that pass five minutes brings the medication that stops status epilepticus.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: U.S. CDC — first aid for seizures — http://cdc.gov/epilepsy/first-aid-for-seizures/

### 18. Treat low blood sugar with fast sugar and recheck after fifteen minutes
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Glucose tablets and juice kept on hand.
- In plain words: A person with diabetes who turns shaky, sweaty, confused, and pale may have low blood sugar. Give about fifteen grams of fast sugar — four glucose tablets and half a cup of juice — then wait fifteen minutes and recheck. When still low, repeat. When the person cannot swallow and loses consciousness, pour nothing into the mouth; call 911.
- Benefit: Fifteen grams of fast-acting sugar raises blood glucose within fifteen minutes and reverses most mild episodes; calling 911 when the person cannot swallow brings the glucagon and IV sugar that prevent seizure and coma.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: American Diabetes Association (2025). Standards of Care — hypoglycemia. Diabetes Care. https://doi.org/10.2337/dc25-S006

### 19. Flush chemical splashes with lots of water and strip contaminated clothing
<!-- costs: money=0 time=mid willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: A long rinse at the tap and a change of clothes.
- In plain words: Chemicals splashed in the eyes need immediate flushing with large amounts of clean water, holding the lids open, for a prolonged rinse. For skin splashes, take off the soaked clothing at once and flush the skin with running water. Bring the chemical container and the label to the emergency department.
- Benefit: Prolonged water irrigation dilutes corrosive chemicals and limits tissue damage; removing soaked clothing stops the burn from continuing under the fabric.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: NIOSH — first aid for chemical exposures — http://www.cdc.gov/niosh/npg/firstaid.html

### 20. After an electric shock, break the contact safely and call 911
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a clear head and a dry wooden handle.
- In plain words: When someone is being shocked, do not touch the person directly. Switch off the power at the breaker and the switch first. When you cannot cut the power, push the person away from the source with a dry wooden broom handle and dry rope — never with metal and never with wet hands. Call 911 even when the person looks fine; heart rhythm problems can appear later.
- Benefit: Cutting the power before touching the victim prevents a second casualty; calling 911 after any mains-voltage shock catches the delayed heart rhythm problems that can kill hours later.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: U.S. CDC — what to do to protect yourself from electrical hazards — https://www.cdc.gov/natural-disasters/response/what-to-do-protect-yourself-from-electrical-hazards.html

### 21. Cool a heat-stroke victim fast and call 911
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; cold water and shade.
- In plain words: Hot red dry skin, confusion, fainting, and a soaring temperature after exertion in the heat mean heat stroke — call 911 at once. Move the person to shade, strip excess clothing, and cool aggressively: cold water on the skin, fanning, and ice packs to the neck, armpits, and groin. Give small sips of cool water only when fully alert.
- Benefit: In heat stroke the body's cooling has failed and organs overheat; aggressive cooling started before 911 crews arrive is the main factor that separates survival from death.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Wilderness Medical Society (2024). Clinical practice guidelines for heat illness. Wilderness and Environmental Medicine. https://doi.org/10.1177/10806032241227924; U.S. National Weather Service — heat illness safety — https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat-illness

### 22. Warm a hypothermia victim gently and call 911
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; dry blankets and shelter from wind.
- In plain words: Uncontrollable shivering that stops, clumsiness, confusion, and drowsiness in the cold mean hypothermia — call 911. Get the person out of the wind and out of wet clothing, wrap in dry blankets with a vapour barrier, and give warm sweet drinks only when fully alert. Handle gently: no rubbing of the limbs and no hot water.
- Benefit: Gentle rewarming after calling 911 prevents the heart rhythm collapse that rough handling can trigger in a severely cold victim.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Wilderness Medical Society (2019). Clinical practice guidelines for hypothermia. Wilderness and Environmental Medicine. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wem.2019.10.002; U.S. National Weather Service — cold safety — https://www.weather.gov/safety/cold-during

### 23. Get out of a house fire fast and stay out
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a practiced escape route.
- In plain words: When fire breaks out, get everyone out through the nearest safe route and close doors behind you to slow the smoke. Crawl low under smoke, feel doors with the back of your hand, and use stairs — never the elevator. Once outside, call 911 from a safe distance and stay out; nothing inside is worth a second trip.
- Benefit: Most fire deaths come from smoke inhalation within minutes, not burns; leaving immediately and calling 911 from outside keeps you out of the smoke layer that kills.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: FEMA — home fires — https://www.ready.gov/home-fires

### 24. For a drowning person, reach and throw — do not go in
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a pole, a rope, and a cool head.
- In plain words: A drowning person cannot call for help; reach the person with a pole, a branch, and a towel from the shore and the dock, and throw a rope and a life ring when reaching fails. Call 911 as soon as someone else can take over the rescue attempt. Enter the water yourself only when you are a trained rescuer; a panicking swimmer can pull an untrained rescuer under.
- Benefit: Reach-and-throw rescue keeps the rescuer out of the water, where most would-be rescuers drown; calling 911 early starts the clock on advanced care for the victim.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lifesaving Society of Canada — drowning rescue guidance — https://www.lifesaving.org/public/download/documents/53934

### 25. Remove a tick with fine tweezers and watch the bite
<!-- costs: money=0 time=mid willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: A pair of fine-tipped tweezers; watching the bite for a month.
- In plain words: Grasp a tick with fine-tipped tweezers as close to the skin as possible and pull straight up with steady pressure — no twisting and no squeezing of the body. Clean the bite with soap and water, save the tick in a dated container, and watch the skin for a spreading bull's-eye rash over the next month. Call your provincial health line for advice on preventive treatment.
- Benefit: Proper removal within hours lowers the chance of Lyme disease transmission; a saved tick lets the lab identify the species and guide preventive antibiotics.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Parks Canada — ticks and Lyme disease — https://parks.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/qc/canallachine/securite-safety/terrestre-land/tiques-lyme-ticks
- Notes: Quebec callers can use Info-Santé at 811 for advice on preventive treatment after a tick bite.

### 26. Wash an animal bite for fifteen minutes and seek rabies shots the same day
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Soap, water, and an emergency visit.
- In plain words: Any bite and scratch that breaks the skin from a bat, a raccoon, a fox, and a stray dog needs immediate washing with soap and running water for fifteen minutes. Then get to emergency care the same day for rabies shots — once symptoms start, rabies cannot be cured. Report the animal to local animal control so it can be found and tested.
- Benefit: Fifteen minutes of soap-and-water washing removes most of the virus from the wound; the same-day vaccine series given before symptoms appear prevents a disease that is otherwise fatal.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Canadian Food Inspection Agency — rabies fact sheet — http://inspection.canada.ca/en/animal-health/terrestrial-animals/diseases/reportable/rabies/fact-sheet

### 27. In a robbery, cooperate and be a good witness
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; self-control under threat.
- In plain words: When someone demands your wallet and phone with threats, hand them over. Your life is worth more than anything you carry. Stay calm, do not chase the robber, and try to remember height, clothing, direction of flight, and anything distinctive. Call 911 as soon as it is safe and give the dispatcher every detail.
- Benefit: Cooperation ends most robberies without injury; a calm, detailed 911 report gives police the description that actually leads to arrests.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: York Regional Police — personal safety tips — http://www.yrp.ca/stay-safe/personal-safety
- Notes: Graded high on death because resisting a robbery is a classic trigger for lethal escalation; the value here is in not getting killed.

### 28. After a risky exposure, ask emergency about HIV preventive pills within three days
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: An emergency visit and a month of pills.
- In plain words: After unprotected sex with a partner whose HIV status is unknown, a broken condom, and a needlestick injury, emergency departments can prescribe a month of preventive pills — but only when started within three days of the exposure, and sooner is better. Go the same day, explain what happened, and take every pill for the full four weeks.
- Benefit: The 28-day preventive course started within 72 hours of exposure blocks the virus from taking hold; each day of delay lowers the protection.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Public Health Agency of Canada — HIV biomedical prevention (PrEP and PEP) fact sheet — https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/publications/diseases-conditions/hiv-factsheet-biomedical-prevention-prep-pep.html

### 29. Never pull out an impaled object — stabilize it and call 911
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; bulky padding and stillness.
- In plain words: A knife, a nail, and a branch stuck in the body may be plugging the bleeding. Leave it exactly where it is. Stabilize it with bulky padding and bandages so it cannot move, keep the person still, and call 911. Removing it outside an operating room can unleash bleeding you cannot stop.
- Benefit: Leaving the object in place tamponades damaged vessels until 911 brings surgical care; field removal is a classic cause of preventable death.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: IFRC — International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2025 — https://www.ifrc.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/IFRC%20International%20First%20Aid,%20Resuscitation%20and%20Education%20Guidelines%202025.pdf

### 30. Splint a broken bone where it lies and do not straighten it
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: A rigid splint and padding from whatever is at hand.
- In plain words: For a suspected fracture, keep the limb still in the position you found it. Pad a rigid splint — a magazine, a board, and a folded umbrella — alongside the break and tie it above and below the injury, including the joints on both sides. Do not try to straighten a bent limb. Apply ice wrapped in cloth and get to emergency care.
- Benefit: Splinting as found prevents a broken bone end from cutting vessels and nerves during transport; leaving a deformed limb alone avoids turning a closed fracture into an open one.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: IFRC — International First Aid, Resuscitation and Education Guidelines 2025 — https://www.ifrc.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/IFRC%20International%20First%20Aid,%20Resuscitation%20and%20Education%20Guidelines%202025.pdf

### 31. Know that helping in an emergency will not get you sued for an honest mistake
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=low dimension=freedom -->
- Cost: Nothing; knowing your rights.
- In plain words: Ontario's Good Samaritan Act protects volunteers who give emergency first aid from lawsuits over honest mistakes; only gross negligence is excluded. Quebec's Charter goes further: it requires every person to come to the aid of anyone whose life is in peril, unless helping would endanger the helper and a third person, and unless there is another valid reason.
- Benefit: Ontario's 2001 Good Samaritan Act shields good-faith rescuers from ordinary-negligence claims, and Quebec's Charter of Human Rights and Freedoms (art. 2) frames rescue as a duty — so fear of liability should never freeze you.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Good Samaritan Act, 2001, S.O. 2001, c. 2 — https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/01g02; Charter of Human Rights and Freedoms, CQLR c. C-12, art. 2 — https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/fr/document/lc/C-12/20050617?langCont=en
- Notes: Statute wording paraphrased per the adaptation contract (no translated statutes); the Quebec article 2 duty is quoted from the official English text.

### 32. Call and text 988 when you and someone you love is in suicide crisis
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a call and staying close.
- In plain words: When you and someone you love is thinking about suicide and self-harm, call and text the national crisis line. Trained counsellors answer in English and French, day and night, every day of the year. Stay with the person, remove means of harm where you safely can, and call 911 when danger is immediate.

### 35. Know the colour: Environment Canada's colour-coded weather alerts
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nothing; a glance at the forecast page.
- In plain words: Since November 26, 2025, every Environment Canada weather watch, advisory, and warning carries a colour. Yellow is the most common: moderate, localized impacts — be aware. - Benefit: Since November 26, 2025, the colour tells you the expected impact at a glance — yellow means watch the situation, orange means rethink your plans, red means your plans change now; for tornadoes and blizzards, minutes matter.

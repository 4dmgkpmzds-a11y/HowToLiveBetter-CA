# 3. Don't waste your energy

This section only runs the numbers on energy and time. It looks at four things: how much attention you have each day, how fast you react, how often you slip, and how long it takes to get back after an interruption. Every number was measured directly — from experiments or from following groups of people over time. Nothing is converted into lifespan or death rates.

Most entries here are grade B; that is normal for this field and does not mean the advice is weak. Item 22 is about how to deal with institutions; it cites rules and a government guide, not experiments, so it is graded C. Items 20 and 24 cite mortality and suicide-risk numbers, which are a different yardstick from the rest of this chapter and are not compared with the others.

### 1. Sleep 7 to 8 hours a night; stop treating 6 as enough

<!-- costs: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It costs 1 to 2 hours of the day, moved into sleep. Going to bed earlier takes some resolve.
- In plain words: Two weeks of six-hour nights drags performance down to the level of two straight nights without sleep. The catch is that people cannot feel it: their own sleepiness ratings stop rising after a few days, while their performance keeps falling.
- Benefit: 48 healthy adults aged 21 to 38 slept 6 hours a night for 14 days. Their cognitive performance fell cumulatively to the level of 2 straight nights without sleep. Their self-rated sleepiness rose only in the first few days, then flatlined; the 6-hour and 4-hour groups rated themselves equally sleepy. This was a randomized controlled trial. Separately, pooling 70 studies of short-term sleep loss: lapses in simple attention g = -0.776, reasoning accuracy g = -0.125.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003). The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.2.117>; Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883>
- Notes: Not feeling sleepy is not evidence of having slept enough. People who habitually sleep 6 hours only feel used to it; their performance does not catch up. The pooled part covers whole nights missed. Only one randomized trial supports chronic short sleep.

### 2. Skip coffee after lunch; keep it to the morning

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. For an 11 p.m. bedtime, the cutoff is about 2 p.m.; people used to afternoon coffee need some resolve to skip it.
- In plain words: A large coffee six hours before bed cuts that night's sleep by over an hour on the monitor, yet the sleeper's own diary shows no difference. A regular cup needs to be finished almost nine hours before bed to leave sleep alone.
- Benefit: 400 mg of caffeine 6 hours before bed cut total sleep time measured by instruments by 1.1 to 1.2 hours versus placebo, yet the subjects' own sleep diaries showed no difference — they could not tell. Pooling many studies: caffeine cut total sleep by 45 minutes on average and sleep efficiency by 7%. One cup of coffee (107 mg) had to be finished at least 8.8 hours before bed to leave sleep unaffected.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Drake, Roehrs, Shambroom & Roth (2013). Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed. Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170>; Gardiner et al. (2023). The effect of caffeine on subsequent sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Sleep Medicine Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764>
- Notes: The Drake trial is a single lab study using 400 mg — a large dose, about two or three coffees. Slow caffeine metabolizers need an even earlier cutoff. What less caffeine buys back is sleep duration, stacking with item 1 (sleep 7 to 8 hours a night).

### 3. Keep the phone out of the bedroom at night

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is resisting "one more episode" night after night. Keeping the phone out of the bedroom takes the least effort.
- In plain words: People who scroll on a phone in bed are more than twice as likely to sleep too little and nearly three times as likely to feel sleepy in the day. A device just sitting in the bedroom, unused, shows nearly the same numbers. The laziest fix is keeping the phone out of the bedroom.
- Benefit: Pooling 20 cross-sectional surveys of 125,198 children and adolescents: bedtime users of portable devices were about 2.17 times as likely to sleep too little (odds ratio 2.17), 1.46 times as likely to report poor sleep quality (odds ratio 1.46), and 2.72 times as likely to report daytime sleepiness (odds ratio 2.72). Merely having a device in the bedroom, unused, gave similar figures: about 1.79, 1.53 and 2.27. A separate survey of 844 adults aged 18 to 94: those who still used a phone after lights-out fell asleep more slowly, spent less of their time in bed actually asleep, and felt more tired in the day.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Carter et al. (2016). Association Between Portable Screen-Based Media Device Access or Use and Sleep Outcomes. JAMA Pediatrics. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341>; Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037>; Grubbs JB et al. (2018). Pornography Problems Due to Moral Incongruence: An Integrative Model with a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. Archives of Sexual Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-018-1248-x>; Prause N, Pfaus J (2015). Viewing Sexual Stimuli Associated with Greater Sexual Responsiveness, Not Erectile Dysfunction. Sexual Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1002/sm2.58>
- Notes: Disputed. The cost of staying up late itself is item 1 (sleep 7 to 8 hours a night). Whether porn content itself harms ordinary people's energy or cognition has no reliable causal evidence. One pooled review argues "porn problems" come mostly from a mismatch between a person's values and their own behaviour, not from the content itself. Another study pooling 280 men found no link between viewing time and erectile function with a partner. This item targets only the sleep time it displaces.

### 4. Close the door when you do real work; cut interruptions

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Close the door, put on headphones, or hang a do-not-disturb sign.
- In plain words: An interruption averaging under three seconds doubles the error rate on what comes next; four and a half seconds triples it. Field tracking found it takes about twenty-five minutes to return to the interrupted task, with two other tasks landing in between.
- Benefit: An average 2.8-second interruption doubled the step-error rate on sequential tasks; a 4.4-second one tripled it. This was in the lab. A field study of 24 office workers: they switched topics every 11 minutes on average, 57% of topics were interrupted, and returning to the interrupted topic took 25 minutes 26 seconds on average, with 2.26 other tasks in between. A 48-person experiment: interrupted people worked faster, but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Altmann, Trafton & Hambrick (2014). Momentary interruptions can derail the train of thought. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030986>; Mark, Gonzalez & Harris (2005). No task left behind? Examining the nature of fragmented work. CHI 2005. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055017>; Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: More speed and stress. CHI 2008. <https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357072>
- Notes: The field study split interruptions into two kinds: other people coming to you, and yourself opening things. A closed door only blocks the first kind. The second kind needs item 8 (notifications off) and item 10 (batch messages) alongside.

### 5. Move your body, get daylight, keep a schedule, call 988 when it gets dark

<!-- costs: money=0 time=mid willpower=some benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free, or nearly free. A few brisk walks, jogs or strength sessions a week. Daylight during the day. A fixed sleep schedule. One phone call when thoughts turn dark: 988.
- In plain words: For low mood, brisk walking and jogging help most, yoga and strength training a little less, and harder beats easier. Light therapy works strongly for seasonal low mood and also for the non-seasonal kind. Guided online self-help courses for low mood work, with a human guide helping more when symptoms are heavier. When thoughts turn to suicide, call 988 first.
- Benefit: Pooling 218 randomized trials with 14,170 people (controls doing other activities): walking or jogging against depression g = -0.62 (95% CI -0.80 to -0.45); yoga -0.55; strength training -0.49; harder worked better. Pooling light-therapy studies: seasonal depression 0.84 (0.60 to 1.08), non-seasonal 0.53 (0.18 to 0.89). A patient-level pooled analysis of internet-based cognitive behavioural therapy: guided and unguided both beat controls; with moderate-to-severe symptoms (PHQ-9 above 9), guided worked better. 988 is Canada's national suicide crisis helpline: call or text, English and French, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, launched November 30, 2023.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Noetel M et al. (2024). Effect of exercise for depression: systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847>; Golden RN et al. (2005). The efficacy of light therapy in the treatment of mood disorders: a review and meta-analysis of the evidence. American Journal of Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656>; Karyotaki E et al. (2021). Internet-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Depression: A Systematic Review and Individual Patient Data Network Meta-analysis. JAMA Psychiatry. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.4364>; Government of Canada (2023). 9-8-8 Suicide Crisis Helpline launches. <https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/news/2023/11/9-8-8-suicide-crisis-helpline.html>; 9-8-8 Suicide Crisis Helpline. <https://988.ca>
- Notes: These are aids for mild-to-moderate low mood. Moderate-to-severe depression, or any suicidal thoughts, need a doctor — call 988 first. Exercise trials were mostly small and cannot blind who exercises. Light-therapy research stops at 2003 and is also small. "Sleep on schedule" and "talk to someone" are the author's advice, without dedicated citations. Do not turn "go move" into a demand on a depressed person; a ten-minute walk counts. 988 operates in English and French only; people who need another language should go through a local crisis line or emergency services.

### 6. Quit comparing your life to what people post online

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Setting a limit or deactivating an account takes minutes. The hard part is not opening it again, which takes long-term resolve.
- In plain words: People who habitually compare themselves upward online tend to feel worse and think less of themselves. People who deactivated Facebook for four weeks felt slightly better but knew less about current events. Students who capped three platforms at ten minutes a day each felt less lonely and less low after three weeks.
- Benefit: Pooling 54 independent samples, 94 effect sizes, N = 36,583: online upward social comparison correlated with poorer psychological adjustment at r = 0.330. Breaking it down: negative evaluative emotions 0.438, anxiety 0.382, depression 0.306, lower well-being 0.268, lower self-esteem 0.263. Age, culture and year did not change the link; long-term studies were no stronger than snapshots. A randomized trial of 2,743 US Facebook users: four weeks deactivated freed 60 minutes a day; subjective well-being rose 0.09 standard deviations (life satisfaction 0.12, anxiety 0.10, depression 0.09, happiness 0.08), about 25% to 40% of a standard positive-psychology intervention; news knowledge fell 0.19 standard deviations. Another randomized trial of 143 undergraduates: capping Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat at 10 minutes a day each cut loneliness and depression after three weeks; anxiety and fear of missing out fell in both groups.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lei Y, Hu S, Sun Y, Zheng L (2026). "Looking up" linked to feeling down: a meta-analysis of online upward social comparison and psychological maladjustment. Frontiers in Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1825169>; Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review, 110(3), 629–676. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658>; Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2019). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. NBER Working Paper No. 25514 <https://www.nber.org/papers/w25514>; Hunt MG, Marx R, Lipson C, Young J (2018). No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751–768. <https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751>
- Notes: The correlation half only shows two things moving together; it cannot be read as cause, and the authors call for more long-term studies. The randomized half can claim cause, but its subjects were US Facebook users before the 2018 midterms — applying it to other platforms is a stretch. The Hunt trial had only 143 undergraduates, and it is the only one. The effects are small; do not expect quitting apps to fix mood. What gets blocked is the "watching how others are doing" kind of use — keeping in touch, looking things up, job hunting are not in scope. If willpower fails, start by turning off push, "people you may know", and home-page recommendations. The total screen-time math is items 6 and 7 in the "Don't waste your time" chapter. For low mood, see item 5 in this chapter. Shopping to fix mood or status is item 24 in the Blacklist chapter; spending to move one rung up is item 28 there.

### 7. Stop working past about 49 hours a week

<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Possibly some overtime pay forgone. It may also take effort to negotiate with a boss.
- In plain words: British munitions-factory records from the First World War show output tracking hours up to about forty-nine a week, then flattening: a seventy-hour week produced barely more than a fifty-six-hour one. The extra hours were worked for nothing.
- Benefit: Output records of British munitions workers in WWI: up to 49 hours a week, output rose in proportion to hours. Past 49, output growth slowed; it peaked around 63 hours. A 70-hour week produced almost the same as a 56-hour week — the extra 14 hours were worked for nothing.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Pencavel (2015). The Productivity of Working Hours. The Economic Journal. <https://doi.org/10.1111/ecoj.12166>
- Notes: The data come from century-old factories — physical work, mostly women. The threshold may differ for knowledge work; the author says other jobs could be higher or lower. What is reliable is the direction: past some point, more hours barely add output. The exact 49 is not reliable.

### 8. Turn off notifications; keep the phone out of sight

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes changing settings; almost no resolve needed.
- In plain words: One notification, unanswered and untouched, still drags down scores on attention tasks — roughly matching the cost of actually taking the call. Texting back costs about the same. And merely having your own phone on the desk, successfully ignored, shrinks the mind's working margin. The more phone-dependent the person, the bigger the drop.
- Benefit: Merely receiving a notification — not looked at, not touched — measurably worsened attention-task scores, by about as much as actually answering a call or texting. This was in the lab with university students. Two further experiments: even while successfully resisting the phone, having your own phone beside you reduced available cognitive capacity. The most phone-dependent people dropped the most.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100>; Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain Drain: The Mere Presence of One's Own Smartphone Reduces Available Cognitive Capacity. Journal of the Association for Consumer Research. <https://doi.org/10.1086/691462>
- Notes: These are single lab tasks. Nobody has measured what daily accumulation does. For jobs that truly need you on call, keep notifications for a few contacts only.

### 9. Wake up at the same time every day

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes some resolve to skip the weekend lie-in.
- In plain words: The most irregular fifth of sleepers ran about two and a half hours later than the most regular fifth — a permanent jet lag. Students with steadier schedules also did better academically.
- Benefit: 61 university students logged 30 days. The most irregular fifth had circadian timing about 2.5 hours later than the most regular fifth: melatonin onset 00:08 versus 21:32. Their sleepiest window also ran about 1.8 hours later. Steadier schedules went with better grades (r = 0.37).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Phillips et al. (2017). Irregular sleep/wake patterns are associated with poorer academic performance and delayed circadian and sleep/wake timing. Scientific Reports. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-03171-4>
- Notes: A single small study, no control group. The researchers infer the circadian difference comes mostly from different daily light exposure — so a fixed wake time needs morning light to work. For catching up after an occasional late night, see the "Don't die slowly" chapter: go to bed earlier that night instead of sleeping in the next morning.

### 10. Check email at fixed times; stop living in the inbox

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes some time to tell colleagues how often you reply, and some resolve not to peek at email constantly.
- In plain words: The same people compared across two weeks: in the week limited to checking email three times a day, daily stress was clearly lower, while the number of emails sent and received stayed the same. Left unlimited, people checked more than fifteen times a day on average.
- Benefit: 124 adults, two weeks, same people compared with themselves. In the week capped at 3 email checks a day, daily stress was clearly lower than in the unlimited week (Cohen's d = 0.37). Emails received and sent did not differ between the weeks. Unrestricted at baseline, people checked email 15.5 times a day on average.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Kushlev & Dunn (2015). Checking email less frequently reduces stress. Computers in Human Behavior. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.11.005>
- Notes: A single study, medium-to-small effect, measuring stress rather than output. Instant messaging has no direct study; the extension is the author's inference.

### 11. Do one thing at a time; quit "training" yourself to multitask

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes some resolve not to answer messages mid-meeting.
- In plain words: Every task switch makes reactions slower and errors more likely. People who habitually keep several screens going have not trained themselves to multitask; they actually score worse on task-switching tests.
- Benefit: After a task switch, reactions are clearly slower and errors more likely — from a review of many studies. In a lab study of university students, heavy media multitaskers were more easily pulled off course by irrelevant things and irrelevant memories, and scored worse on task-switching tests.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Monsell (2003). Task switching. Trends in Cognitive Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00028-7>; Ophir, Nass & Wagner (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106>
- Notes: How much extra task-switching costs varies a lot by task; no single number is given here. The Ophir study is a same-time-point comparison — it cannot tell whether multitasking worsens attention or people with worse attention multitask more.

### 12. Read paper before bed; dim the screen an hour early

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Putting the phone down for the last hour before bed takes long-term resolve against habit.
- In plain words: Reading on a glowing screen before bed delays sleep by about ten minutes versus paper and pushes the body clock back by over an hour and a half. Melatonin drops by more than half, mornings feel groggier, and full alertness takes longer.
- Benefit: In a crossover lab trial, reading a light-emitting e-reader before bed versus a paper book: 25.7 minutes to fall asleep versus 15.8 (about 10 minutes slower). Evening melatonin suppressed by 55%. Next-day circadian timing delayed by over 1.5 hours (melatonin onset 22:31 versus 21:01). Next morning felt sleepier and took longer to fully wake.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. PNAS. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1418490112>
- Notes: A small lab study under controlled conditions. With everyday phone brightness and usage, the effect may be smaller. Whether night mode helps was not tested.

### 13. Keep naps to about 10 minutes

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Ten minutes, plus somewhere to lie down or lean.
- In plain words: A ten-minute nap perks people up on the spot, with the lift lasting a couple of hours. Five minutes does almost nothing. Twenty minutes needs half an hour before it pays off. Thirty minutes starts with grogginess, during which performance is worse than no nap at all.
- Benefit: After a short night, a 10-minute nap immediately improved sleepiness, fatigue, energy and mental performance, with some benefits lasting past 155 minutes. A 5-minute nap gave almost nothing. A 20-minute nap's benefits appeared only after 35 minutes. A 30-minute nap began with sleep inertia: performance right after waking was worse than no nap, then improved.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Brooks & Lack (2006). A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: Which nap duration is most recuperative? Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.6.831>
- Notes: A single lab study, all healthy young subjects. Do not force it if you cannot sleep. Whether lying with eyes closed for 10 minutes helps has no support. Naps over an hour are linked with higher mortality and heart risk instead — see the "Don't die slowly" chapter.

### 14. Catch yourself replaying the incident; switch to something hands-on

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is noticing the replay loop in the first place — that noticing takes practice.
- In plain words: Replaying the same bad moment never produces an answer. It deepens low mood, cements negative thoughts, weakens problem-solving, blocks real action, and wears out the people willing to listen. Once you catch yourself going in circles, switch to something hands-on.
- Benefit: A review of many studies: replaying the same bad moment (rumination) deepens depression. It also entrenches negative thinking, weakens problem-solving, blocks real action, and wears out willing listeners. It is linked with anxiety, binge eating, heavy drinking and self-harm.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco & Lyubomirsky (2008). Rethinking Rumination. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x>
- Notes: The review gives directions, not numbers. "Switch to something hands-on" is the author's own practice. What the literature supports is one line: diverting attention beats continuing to circle. Nobody has compared what to switch to.

### 15. Subtract the draining people first, then add the supportive ones

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. There will be short-term social friction and possibly lost relationships in the near term. Refusing takes real resolve.
- In plain words: The damage from one draining person outweighs, and is more consistent than, the benefit from one supportive person. So subtract first, then add.
- Benefit: A survey of 120 widowed women aged 60 to 89: unpleasant dealings tracked psychological well-being more consistently and more strongly than pleasant ones. By that result, one draining person costs more than one supportive person gives.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Rook (1984). The negative side of social interaction: Impact on psychological well-being. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.5.1097>
- Notes: The respondents are a special group, and it is a one-time survey — no before/after. It measured well-being, not energy. Placing it here is the author's inference. There is no objective standard for which relationships count as "draining"; that call is yours.

### 16. When angry, walk away; do not try to win the argument on the spot

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes practice to hold back the retort on the spot, walk away, and decide later whether to act at all.
- In plain words: Reframing what just happened — seeing it from the other side — measurably brings feelings down; the second works better. Clamping down on expression does nothing. And people who punished an offender kept replaying the incident longer afterward; those who let it go moved on faster. The satisfaction of revenge is overestimated in advance.
- Benefit: Pooling 306 experiments: reframing what happened (cognitive reappraisal) moved emotions at d = 0.36; taking the other side's perspective at d = 0.45; distraction at d = 0.27; suppressing expression did nothing (d = -0.04). Three experiments: people who punished an offender kept replaying it longer; non-punishers turned the page faster. People overestimate revenge's payoff in advance.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Webb TL, Miles E, Sheeran P (2012). Dealing with feeling: A meta-analysis of the effectiveness of strategies derived from the process model of emotion regulation. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600>; Carlsmith KM, Wilson TD, Gilbert DT (2008). The paradoxical consequences of revenge. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012165>
- Notes: The pooled part measured emotion intensity, not energy; effects small to medium. The revenge part is a single set of experiments, hence the B. Where someone caused you a real loss, the formal complaint route costs less than exploding on the spot.

### 17. Cool anger by calming down, not by venting it

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. In the heat of the moment, switch to a few minutes of whatever slows the heartbeat: slow breathing, sitting down, stillness. The hard part is that venting is exactly what you want to do.
- In plain words: Punching a bag, smashing things, going for a run — arousing "let it out" methods do not reduce anger overall. Calming methods like slow breathing and meditation clearly press down anger and the urge to act on it.
- Benefit: Pooling 154 studies, 184 samples, 10,189 people: calming methods (deep breathing, mindfulness, meditation) lowered anger and aggression at g = -0.63 (95% CI -0.82 to -0.43), stable across sex, race, age and culture. Arousing methods (punching bags, jogging, cycling) did nothing overall, g = -0.02 (-0.13 to 0.09). The authors' conclusion: venting anger or "running it off" are not working ways to cool down.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Kjærvik SL, Bushman BJ (2024). A meta-analytic review of anger management activities that increase or decrease arousal: What fuels or douses rage? Clinical Psychology Review. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2024.102414>
- Notes: Anger and aggression were measured, not energy; the time yardstick is the author's classification — one fewer fight means one fewer mess to clean up. For in-the-moment calming techniques see the chapter on how to rest, and item 16 in this chapter (walk away when angry). Running is not useless: long-term it helps low mood (see the chapter on recovering from a major blow). It is just not an in-the-moment anger tool. Angry: do not drive, do not send messages, do not decide. If someone is hitting you, leave and call the police — that is not an emotion-management problem.

### 18. Make important calls after sleep, not while sleep-deprived

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It only pushes things to after sleep.
- In plain words: Well-rested people quickly learn to dodge high-risk options. After two straight nights without sleep, people kept picking the risky ones — and the older the person, the stronger the effect. Nobody has measured what a few short hours do, but important calls still belong after sleep.
- Benefit: 34 healthy people went 49.5 hours without sleep, then did the Iowa Gambling Task, a lab measure of risky choice. Rested, they quickly learned to avoid high-risk options. Sleep-deprived, they chose high-risk options more, and older people were hit harder. One experiment, only one study.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Killgore, Balkin & Wesensten (2006). Impaired decision making following 49 h of sleep deprivation. Journal of Sleep Research. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00487.x>
- Notes: An extreme case: two straight nights without sleep. What 2 a.m. does to ordinary people was never directly measured. The "don't send messages" half is the author's advice (grade C); sending it the next day costs nothing.

### 19. Wear earplugs or grab a quiet room for focused work

<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=low dimension=time -->
- Cost: Foam earplugs are cheap. Booking a quiet meeting room takes a little time.
- In plain words: Raising background noise from the high thirties to the low fifties of decibels shrinks the words people remember, tires them out, and makes them want to quit. Resting to a nature film with sound restores energy better than resting to office noise.
- Benefit: Students worked in a simulated open office. When background noise rose from 39 dB to 51 dB (LAeq), they remembered fewer words, rated themselves more tired, and wanted to work less. Resting to a nature film with sound restored their energy ratings better than river sounds alone or office noise.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Jahncke, Hygge, Halin, Green & Dimberg (2011). Open-plan office noise: Cognitive performance and restoration. Journal of Environmental Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2011.07.002>
- Notes: A single lab study. The most disruptive noise is intelligible speech. White-noise earphones help with that, but this study did not test earphones.

### 20. Stop treating a pessimistic forecast as an accurate prediction

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is treating this kind of thought as a symptom rather than as fact.
- In plain words: In a twenty-year twin study, each step up in pessimism came with about a tenth more deaths in the same period and about a fifth more cardiovascular deaths. Higher optimism scores brought no matching benefit. So the thing to handle is pessimism, not a lack of positivity.
- Benefit: 2,978 Australian twins aged 50 and up, followed 20 years on average; 1,068 died. Each standard deviation higher on the pessimism scale came with about 13% higher mortality in the same period (hazard ratio 1.134, 95% CI 1.065-1.207) and about 20% higher cardiovascular death (1.196, 1.045-1.368). No link with cancer death. Optimism scores showed no clear link with mortality either.
- Evidence: B (disputed)
- Sources: Whitfield JB, Zhu G, Landers JG, Martin NG (2020). Pessimism is associated with greater all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, but optimism is not protective. Scientific Reports, 10, 12609. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-69388-y>
- Notes: Disputed. This is observation only, no grouping. One could argue sickness came first and caused the pessimism; the authors counter that cancer deaths should then also correlate. Unmeasured factors may still remain. Optimism showed no protective effect, so do not force "positive thinking". The work is to stop treating pessimistic forecasts as accurate predictions of the future.

### 21. Fix the trivial defaults: what to wear, what to eat

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=low dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes a little time up front to fix the defaults.
- In plain words: There is no hard evidence for this one. The original "choices drain willpower" experiment failed to replicate in two large pre-registered redos — one with about two thousand people, one with about three thousand — across many labs, with effects near zero. Fixing what you wear and eat is still worth doing, for the minutes and the dithering it saves, not for "saving willpower."
- Benefit: Cannot be quantified. The original experiment claimed choosing and self-control draw on one shared limited resource (ego depletion). Two later large pre-registered replications, run as originally designed across many labs, did not reproduce it: 23 labs and 2,141 people, d = 0.04 (95% CI -0.07 to 0.15); 36 labs and 3,531 people, d = 0.06 — too small to count.
- Evidence: C (disputed)
- Sources: Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven & Tice (1998). Ego depletion: Is the active self a limited resource? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1252>; Hagger et al. (2016). A Multilab Preregistered Replication of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873>; Vohs et al. (2021). A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Psychological Science. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797621989733>
- Notes: Disputed. "Decision fatigue" is treated as settled in popular books, but later direct replications mostly failed. It is still worth doing: near-zero cost, and the minutes saved are real. Do not expect it to improve your afternoon judgment.

### 22. Treat police officers, doctors and counter staff as people working by the rules, not as roles

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. What changes is your expectation and the order of your actions. The first time, you have to resist the urge to argue until the other side agrees.
- In plain words: Police officers, doctors and counter staff are people working a post by the rules, managed by paperwork, deadlines and oversight. Whether they are nice to you has little to do with whether your matter moves and how many days it takes. So get receipts, note deadlines, and use the proper channel — instead of trying to argue someone into agreement.
- Benefit: Swap the moves: from pleading, anger and re-telling the grievance, to getting documents, noting deadlines, and appealing through the proper channel. What is saved is repeated back-and-forth and replaying it afterward. This is a one-time adjustment of expectations, not daily time saved.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Ontario Ombudsman. What to gather before making a complaint. <https://www.ombudsman.on.ca/en/make-complaint/what-you-can-expect/how-make-complaint>
- Notes: Rated C because the claim that people treat counter staff as roles rather than workers has no citable literature. What can be cited is the procedural half: the Ontario Ombudsman's guide lists what to gather before complaining — names and titles, dates, and written communication. A paper trail does not fix a rude attitude and does not guarantee the outcome; it changes your expectation and your first move.

### 23. Halve your estimate when it feels like everyone saw you embarrass yourself

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Just one extra question in the tense moment: did they really notice.
- In plain words: Wearing an embarrassing T-shirt into a room, the wearer guessed nearly half the onlookers could name the face on it; under a quarter actually could. Wearing a T-shirt they felt good in, people guessed six times the true number. After conversations, people also systematically underestimate how much the other person liked them. So when it feels like everyone remembers your embarrassment, even half your estimate is still too high.
- Benefit: Experiment one: participants wore a T-shirt with an embarrassing face into a room of onlookers. Wearers estimated 46% of onlookers could name the face; the actual rate was 23% — the estimate was exactly double. Experiment two: wearing a T-shirt they chose and felt good in, wearers estimated 48% while the actual rate was 8% — six times. A separate line of research across three settings — strangers paired for conversation, freshman roommates, adult workshops — found people systematically underestimate how much their conversation partner liked them and enjoyed the conversation: the "liking gap". It appeared at every conversation length and lasted months between roommates.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Gilovich T, Medvec VH, Savitsky K (2000). The spotlight effect in social judgment: An egocentric bias in estimates of the salience of one's own actions and appearance. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78(2), 211–222. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211>; Boothby EJ, Cooney G, Sandstrom GM, Clark MS (2018). The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think? Psychological Science, 29(11), 1742–1756. <https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714>
- Notes: Rated B because these are small lab and campus studies — T-shirts and chats with strangers. There is no direct evidence for "colleagues gossiping about me" or "misspeaking in a meeting". The numbers show the direction and the rough multiple, not a coefficient to apply. Other people's views are not all unimportant: in real evaluations — interviews, exams, performance reviews — the judgment is real, so prepare for those. What gets discounted is the everyday feeling that everyone is watching; that part is your own estimation bias. Where someone caused you a real loss, the formal complaint route costs less than exploding on the spot.

### 24. Stop demanding the thing that was never going to happen

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is updating an old belief about how the world owes you something.
- In plain words: Most resentment is a gap between what you assumed you were owed and what actually happened. Once the "owed" part is seen as an assumption, the event itself is just something that happened. The anger has nothing left to stand on.
- Benefit: Unquantifiable. In a survey of 3,806 twins: feeling mistreated lowered well-being at r = -0.32; feeling gratitude raised it at r = 0.16. Those who saw their own effort behind good outcomes were more grateful (r = 0.14); those who blamed others for bad outcomes felt more victimized (r = 0.20). The link between a bad event and well-being ran through how fair the person judged the event to be.
- Evidence: B (disputed)
- Sources: Smith MM, Sherry SB, Chen S, Saklofske DH, Mushquash C, Flett GL, Hewitt PL (2018). The perniciousness of perfectionism: A meta-analytic review of the perfectionism-suicide relationship. Journal of Personality, 86(3), 522–542. <https://doi.org/10.1111/jopy.12333>
- Notes: Rated B because most included studies were one-time surveys — no before/after. The correlation is middling (r around .28), explaining only several to ten percent of the variance. It measured suicidal thoughts and attempts, not death itself, so the benefit is graded small as a proxy endpoint. The authors note likely publication bias in the perfectionism-concern/attempt group — unpublished negative results. Filling in the estimated missing studies leaves the direction unchanged. High standards are not the problem; do not lower them for this. What matters is where the standard comes from, and whether falling short feels like "I am a failure". Like the pessimistic forecasts in item 20, this is something treatable as a symptom. With suicidal thoughts, call 988 first and move lethal means away.

### 25. Write down your thoughts and feelings for a few days, but don't expect it to cure anything

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Fifteen to twenty minutes a day for three to four days.
- In plain words: Writing down an upsetting event shows, on average, a small positive effect — but two stricter pooled reviews found no clear effect. It costs almost nothing, so it is worth a try. If the problem is severe, see a clinician instead.
- Benefit: Three pooled reviews of expressive writing. The first pooled 146 randomized trials and found a positive but small average effect, r = .075. The second pooled 30 randomized trials and found no clear effect on physical or psychological health. The third pooled 16 randomized trials in cancer patients and survivors and found no clear effect on psychological health, physical health, or quality of life (psychological g = 0.04, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.14). The studies' usual protocol: 3 to 4 sessions of 15 to 20 minutes, writing the deepest thoughts and feelings about an upsetting event.
- Evidence: B (disputed)
- Sources: Frattaroli J (2006). Experimental disclosure and its moderators: a meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823>; Mogk C, Otte S, Reinhold-Hurley B, Kröner-Herwig B (2006). Health effects of expressive writing on stressful or traumatic experiences - a meta-analysis. Psycho-Social Medicine. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19742069/>; Zachariae R, O'Toole MS (2015). The effect of expressive writing intervention on psychological and physical health outcomes in cancer patients--a systematic review and meta-analysis. Psycho-Oncology. <https://doi.org/10.1002/pon.3802>
- Notes: Disputed. The supporting side is a pooled review of 146 studies with a positive but small effect; the opposing side is two pooled reviews restricted to randomized trials, both finding no clear effect. The 3-to-4-session, 15-to-20-minute protocol comes from the earliest experimental design, described in the Mogk review's abstract. If writing spirals into worse distress, stop and call 988. For low mood that does not lift, see item 5 in this chapter.

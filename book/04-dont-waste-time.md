# 4. Don't waste your time

This section only runs the numbers on time: hours saved per day, week, or year, or how much on-time completion improves. Nothing is converted into lifespan or money. Where money appears, only the method is given, never the answer. Most entries are graded B or C, because most rest on single studies or on time-use survey totals. That is normal for behavioural research, not a sign the advice is weak.

### 1. Turn "I plan to do it" into "at what time, where, doing what when X happens"

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Each goal takes 1 to 2 minutes: write one "if…then…" sentence. For example: "Tuesday at 8 a.m., when I sit at the desk, I start with the introduction."
- In plain words: Rewriting "I will do this" as "Tuesday at 8 a.m., when I sit at the desk, I start with the introduction" raises the rate at which goals get done, across dozens of independent tests, by a medium-to-large amount. Only the wording of one sentence changes; it costs no money and no resolve.
- Benefit: Pooling 94 independent tests, writing a goal first as an "if…then…" plan raised goal achievement. In psychology this is called an implementation intention. Effect size d = 0.65, medium to large. It helped in three places: getting started, holding up when interrupted, and walking away from things that cannot work.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: It only works on things you actually want to do. If you do not want the goal, no number of sentences helps. Also, these studies mostly used students and mostly looked at health habits — carrying them to work settings is extrapolation.

### 2. Write your exit conditions before you start

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Five minutes at the start, to write "if <metric> isn't met by <date>, I stop." The hard part is honouring it on the day, in front of people.
- In plain words: Before starting, write one sentence: if some target is not met by some date, stop. Pooling many studies, this kind of pre-written "if…then…" really does help people walk away from courses of action that cannot work. What it saves is all the time you would otherwise keep pouring in.
- Benefit: Pooling many implementation-intention studies, a pre-set "if…then…" helped people disengage from failing courses of action. The time saved is whatever you would otherwise have kept investing.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>
- Notes: Set the exit conditions at the start, while you are still calm. Set them later and the money already spent will pull the numbers. When judging, count only future investment and future return — see item 3. "Being willing to quit" is only one sub-conclusion of the pooled studies; nobody has measured how much time quitting saves.

### 3. Estimate time from how long similar tasks actually took, not from the plan

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes before estimating, to look up how long the same kind of task actually took last time.
- In plain words: Students estimated they would finish a thesis in about thirty-four days; it took fifty-six, and fewer than a third finished on their own estimate. Another group was asked to recall how long the same kind of task took them before, and their on-time rate doubled from about twenty-nine percent to sixty percent.
- Benefit: Students predicted 33.9 days to finish a thesis; it took 55.5. Only 29.7% finished on their own estimate. Another group was asked to link the current task to their past experience before estimating. Their on-time completion rate rose from the control group's 29.3% to 60.0%. The optimism that shrinks estimates disappeared in this group.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>; Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. <https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302>; Halkjelsvik, T., & Jørgensen, M. (2012). From origami to software development: A review of studies on judgment-based predictions of performance time. Psychological Bulletin, 138(2), 238–271. <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025996>
- Notes: Buehler's study used only Canadian university students (Waterloo), a single small study — about 37 people in one study and about 40 per group in another. Estimating from the history of similar projects is called reference-class forecasting; how engineers do it is in Flyvbjerg. A review of many studies found underestimating is more common than overestimating, but by how much varies a lot with the task and the method.

### 4. Give tasks without an external deadline a date of your own

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is honouring the date once set — ignoring it on the day is the same as never setting one.
- In plain words: With a deadline set by someone else, about eight in ten students finished before it. On their own estimated time, fewer than four in ten did. When the work got done tracked the deadline almost exactly, and barely tracked their own estimate. So tasks without a deadline need one you set yourself, and you have to honour it.
- Benefit: With an external deadline, 80.6% of students finished before it. Only 38.7% finished within their own estimated time. When the work got done followed the deadline almost exactly (r = 0.82), and had only a weak link to their own estimate.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67(3), 366–381. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366>
- Notes: Tell the date to someone else and it starts to work like an externally set one. To be clear, the studies verified that externally set deadlines work. Self-set ones — timeboxing, Pomodoro — have never been directly tested; this is extrapolation.

### 5. Outsource chores when the price is below your hourly rate

<!-- costs: money=high time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: It costs whatever the service costs, plus one session of math to work out your own hourly rate.
- In plain words: People who spend money to buy time report higher life satisfaction. The same money spent buying time, rather than buying things, brings more happiness in the moment.
- Benefit: A study sampled 6,271 people across four countries: those who spent money to buy time reported higher life satisfaction. The researchers also ran a real-life experiment: the same amount of money spent on buying time rather than on material goods brought higher momentary happiness.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., Dunn, E. W., Smeets, P., Bekkers, R., & Norton, M. I. (2017). Buying time promotes happiness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. <https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>
- Notes: First work out your hourly rate: monthly after-tax income divided by monthly actual working hours. Then look at what the chore costs to outsource. If the outsourcing price is below your hourly rate, and the freed time really goes to earning or resting, outsource it. Everyone's rate and prices differ; the answer has to be computed by you. Also, Whillans measured happiness after buying time, not hours saved.

### 6. Set a hard cap on doomscrolling and short video

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. A few minutes of action: turn off push notifications, delete the app, or set a daily time limit on it. The hard part is keeping your finger from opening it again.
- In plain words: This is the biggest time item in this section. Cutting one hour a day from it equals three hundred and sixty-five hours a year. Online time also includes work, study and keeping in touch — not all of it is waste.
- Benefit: Cutting one hour a day equals 365 hours a year. That arithmetic is the whole benefit; no study has measured how much is actually saved by a cap, or where the freed time goes.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Allcott H, Braghieri L, Eichmeyer S, Gentzkow M (2020). The Welfare Effects of Social Media. American Economic Review, 110(3), 629–676. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658>; Hunt MG, Marx R, Lipson C, Young J (2018). No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 37(10), 751–768. <https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751>
- Notes: The two cited trials are about the "watching how others are doing" kind of use: deactivating Facebook for four weeks freed 60 minutes a day, and capping three platforms at 10 minutes a day each cut loneliness and depression in students. For those, see item 6 in the "Don't waste your energy" chapter. But no study has measured the cap itself.

### 7. Skip TV and rolling news; batch what you need at fixed times

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is breaking the habit of opening it on impulse, and you may miss some instant updates.
- In plain words: In Canada's 2010 time-use survey, people who watched TV averaged almost three hours of it on the diary day. Moving what you actually want to know into fixed, batched viewing saves that whole block.
- Benefit: Statistics Canada's 2010 General Social Survey on time use: TV watchers averaged 2 hours 52 minutes of TV on the diary day. Whatever is not watched is saved in full.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Statistics Canada. General Social Survey 2010: time spent watching television. <https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/89-647-x/2011001/hl-fs-eng.htm>
- Notes: Only totals are on hand. No study has measured where the saved time goes after people watch less TV and news. "Watch it in batches" is the author's experience.

### 8. When deciding whether to continue, count only future cost and future return

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. The hard part is accepting one fact: the time already put in is gone and cannot be recovered.
- In plain words: Money and time already invested make people want to keep going. Pooling dozens of datasets, this tendency is stable. So when judging whether to continue, count only what you still have to put in and what you can still get back. Leave what is already spent out of the math.
- Benefit: Money, effort and time already invested make people more inclined to continue — the sunk-cost effect. Pooling 98 datasets, the tendency is confirmed stable. Avoiding it stops you from pouring in more time just so the earlier spending "won't be wasted".
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Arkes, H. R., & Blumer, C. (1985). The psychology of sunk cost. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35, 124–140. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4>; Roth, S., Robbert, T., & Straus, L. (2015). On the sunk-cost effect in economic decision-making: A meta-analytic review. Business Research, 8(1), 99–138. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s40685-014-0014-8>
- Notes: The theatre experiment by Arkes and Blumer ran like this. An Ohio university theatre sold season tickets, randomly giving buyers full price (15 dollars), 2 dollars off, or 7 dollars off. In the first half of the season, the full-price group attended 4.11 shows on average, the other two groups 3.32 and 3.29. In the second half the three groups no longer differed. Roth and colleagues found the effect fades with time, and older people are less affected.

### 9. No agenda, no meeting; stand when you can

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. The organizer spends 5 to 10 minutes writing an agenda before the meeting. Standing meetings need the attendees to go along.
- In plain words: Meetings with an agenda sent in advance are more likely to be rated useful by the attendees. In an experiment, seated meetings ran about a third longer than standing ones, while the decisions made came out the same.
- Benefit: Two surveys, 958 and 292 people. Meetings with an agenda sent in advance were more likely to be rated effective by attendees, a link that holds up statistically. In another experiment, 55 groups met seated and 56 standing; the seated ones ran 34% longer. The quality of decisions did not differ between the two formats.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Leach, D. J., Rogelberg, S. G., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2009). Perceived meeting effectiveness: The role of design characteristics. Journal of Business and Psychology, 24(1), 65–76. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10869-009-9092-6>; Bluedorn, A. C., Turban, D. B., & Love, M. S. (1999). The effects of stand-up and sit-down meeting formats on meeting outcomes. Journal of Applied Psychology, 84 <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.2.277>
- Notes: The standing-meeting experiment used 5-person groups on lab tasks. Do not copy the conclusion to long meetings or large conferences. Leach measured whether attendees felt the meeting worked, not how much was actually produced.

### 10. Cut the number of meetings; use async text where possible

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It needs colleagues and the boss to go along. The hard part is that writing something clearly takes more effort than saying it out loud.
- In plain words: The more meetings in a day, the more tired people are and the heavier their workload feels. Cutting one meeting saves exactly that meeting's length, with no discount.
- Benefit: Two surveys of employees working 35 hours a week or more, 676 and 304 people. How much time meetings take had no fixed effect on work attitudes and well-being — it depended on two things: how good the meetings were, and how much the job depended on other people. Another study had people keep a week-long diary: the more meetings in a day, the higher the fatigue and the heavier the felt workload. Time saved equals the length of the meeting you cut.
- Evidence: B (disputed)
- Sources: Rogelberg, S. G., Leach, D. J., Warr, P. B., & Burnfield, J. L. (2006). "Not another meeting!" Are meeting time demands related to employee well-being? Journal of Applied Psychology, 91 <https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.91.1.83>; Luong, A., & Rogelberg, S. G. (2005). Meetings and more meetings: The relationship between meeting load and the daily well-being of employees. Group Dynamics: Theory, Research, and Practice, 9 <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2699.9.1.58>
- Notes: Disputed. Rogelberg 2006 found the link between meeting load and well-being is not one-sided: in jobs that heavily depend on other people, more meetings are not a bad thing. Also, no study has directly compared "switch to async text" against meeting — that part is the author's inference.

### 11. Break big tasks into subtasks before estimating and starting

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Breaking it down takes 5 to 15 minutes.
- In plain words: When estimating, people do not automatically unpack a task into its parts. Listing the subtasks first shortens the estimate's shortfall. And once unpacked, each small step is something you can start right away, which also cuts procrastination.
- Benefit: When estimating, people do not spontaneously break a multi-part task — writing a literature review, a discussion, a reference list — into pieces. Listing the subtasks first reduces the planning-fallacy shortfall. Each small step then becomes an action you can start immediately, and procrastination drops with it. Among the strongest predictors of procrastination are dislike of the task itself and pushing the task back.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Kruger, J., & Evans, M. (2004). If you don't want to be late, enumerate: Unpacking reduces the planning fallacy. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 40(5), 586–598. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2003.11.001>; Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133 <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: Break it down to the level where each subtask fits in one sitting; splitting too finely wastes time on its own. Also, Kruger and Evans was only verified from the abstract for direction — the exact percentages were not checked against the original text.

### 12. Treat procrastination as avoiding discomfort, not as laziness

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. Only the direction of judgment changes: first ask where the task hurts, then fix that.
- In plain words: Procrastination has little to do with laziness, and not much to do with personality either. What best predicts whether someone procrastinates is how aversive the task itself is, how capable they feel of it, and how easily they are pulled away by what is in front of them. So what works is making the task less aversive and the first step smaller — not scolding yourself and pushing harder.
- Benefit: A meta-analysis of 691 correlations found the strong, stable correlates of procrastination: how aversive the task is, how far the deadline is, self-efficacy (feeling capable), impulsiveness, and conscientiousness with its facets of self-control, distractibility, organization and achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation-seeking had only weak links. The author explains the pattern with temporal motivation theory: a task's present appeal shrinks fast as its payoff moves further away.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. Psychological Bulletin, 133 <https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65>
- Notes: The benefit is graded "mid" because these studies only point in a direction — none measured hours saved. The concrete moves are in item 1 of this chapter (write it as "at what time, where, doing what when X happens"), item 11 (break big tasks into subtasks), and item 4 (give tasks without an external deadline a date of your own). These are pooled correlational studies; they cannot show what causes what.

### 13. Put what you need within reach and what tempts you far away

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free, a few minutes once. Put the phone in another room, spread the books and the laptop on the desk.
- In plain words: Move something a little further away and people take less of it. Moving the same snacks further away clearly cut how much got eaten — a medium-sized effect. Fewer options on display also clearly cut how many people took it. These tricks need no willpower in the moment and take minutes to set up.
- Benefit: A Cochrane review of 24 randomized trials. Moving food further away cut how much was eaten: standardized mean difference -0.60 (95% CI -0.84 to -0.36; 12 studies, 1,098 people, low-certainty evidence), a medium effect. Reducing the number of options cut selection of that food more clearly: standardized mean difference -1.13 (95% CI -1.90 to -0.37; 3 studies, 154 people, low-certainty evidence). Regression showed: the further away and the fewer the options, the stronger the effect.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Hollands GJ, Carter P, Anwer S, et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products to change their selection and consumption. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 9, CD012573. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3>
- Notes: The B grade: all 24 included studies were about food — none on alcohol or tobacco; 14 were lab studies, all from high-income countries. So "move it further and you'll touch it less" applied to phones and games is extrapolation with no direct trial. Putting the phone out of sight specifically has been directly tested — see item 8 in the "Don't waste your energy" chapter.

### 14. Put money on the outcome, but first ask whether you'd actually stake it

<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Deposit-style schemes need you to put up your own money first, forfeited if you fail. In the trial the stake was 150 US dollars.
- In plain words: To force yourself to follow through, two designs work: take a bonus on success; put down a deposit, returned with a bonus only on success. Among people willing to join either, the deposit group succeeded by about thirteen percentage points more. But only about one in seven would take the deposit, versus nine in ten for the bonus — so deposits only work for people willing to stake.
- Benefit: A US randomized trial of 2,538 people assigned employees and their family or friends to four incentive schemes or usual care. Reward-type (about 800 US dollars on success) had 90.0% uptake; deposit-type (150 US dollars down, plus 650 dollars reward) had 13.7%. Six-month sustained quit rates: 9.4% to 16.0% across the four incentive schemes, 6.0% for usual care. Reward-type 15.7%, deposit-type 10.2%. But among the 13.7% willing to join either, the deposit type's six-month quit rate beat the reward type by 13.2 percentage points (95% CI 3.1 to 22.8).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Halpern SD, French B, Small DS, et al. (2015). Randomized trial of four financial-incentive programs for smoking cessation. New England Journal of Medicine, 372(22), 2108–2117. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1414293>
- Notes: The trial measured quitting smoking; carrying it to theses or fitness is extrapolation, so the benefit is graded "mid". When choosing a scheme, first check whether you will actually use it. A method most people refuse has no use for those people, however strong its effect. So pick the kind you will accept — a smaller stake beats no stake. On quitting smoking itself, see the "Don't die slowly" chapter.

### 15. Count new habits in months, not in "21 days"

<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. What it takes is repeating the same action in the same setting every day, for months.
- In plain words: "Twenty-one days to build a habit" has no backing. In a twelve-week tracking study, an action becoming automatic took eighteen days at the fast end and two hundred and fifty-four at the slow end. Still finding it hard after two to three weeks is normal. Missing one day does not reset the progress.
- Benefit: 96 volunteers each picked one eating, drinking or activity action, did it daily in a fixed setting (for example "after breakfast") for 12 weeks, and rated their own automaticity daily. 82 people's data were usable; the model fit 62. Days to reach 95% of each person's automaticity ceiling ranged from 18 to 254. The study also found that missing one chance to perform it had no substantial effect on habit formation.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lally P, van Jaarsveld CHM, Potts HWW, Wardle J (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. European Journal of Social Psychology, 40(6), 998–1009. <https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674>
- Notes: Only this one study, 96 people, all simple actions (like eating a piece of fruit after lunch). Nobody has measured how long complex ones take — running three times a week, writing daily. So still finding it hard after two weeks is not evidence of failure. To make the repetition actually happen, tie the action to a fixed setting — see item 1 of this chapter.

### 16. For procrastination that hurts, use self-help with trial backing before seeing a therapist

<!-- costs: money=low time=mid willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: The price of a self-help book, or nothing if you use free materials. It takes about ten weeks, one section and one exercise per week.
- In plain words: Procrastination bad enough to cause suffering is treatable. Ten weeks of cognitive-behavioural self-help materials — with a guide, and fully on your own, clearly beat doing nothing. Guided and unguided showed no difference, so doing it yourself works too.
- Benefit: 150 people randomized into three groups: guided self-help, unguided self-help, waitlist control. After ten weeks, on the pure procrastination scale, between-group effect sizes were d = 0.70 (95% CI 0.29–1.10) for the guided group and d = 0.50 (0.10–0.90) for the unguided group; on the irrational procrastination scale, 0.81 (0.40–1.22) and 0.69 (0.29–1.09). Clinically meaningful improvement: 31.3% to 40.0% guided, 24.0% to 36.0% unguided. The two groups did not differ on any outcome.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Rozental A, Forsell E, Svensson A, Andersson G, Carlbring P (2015). Internet-based cognitive-behavior therapy for procrastination: A randomized controlled trial. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 83(4), 808–824. <https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000023>
- Notes: The subjects were Swedish adults using Swedish-language materials, and this book did not verify an equivalent English-language program — hence the "mid" benefit grade. The materials' components are the usual set: behavioural activation, behavioural experiments, stimulus control, and teaching about motivation and work methods. The stimulus-control part is item 13 of this chapter (put what tempts you far away). If procrastination comes with clearly low mood or anxiety, start with item 5 in the "Don't waste your energy" chapter.

### 17. Spend one session learning the shortcuts of the software you use daily

<!-- costs: money=0 time=mid willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: Free. It takes a one-time investment of several hours to tens of hours to learn. The hard part is that the first stretch is slower than the mouse.
- In plain words: Most people who have used Word for years still click toolbar icons. In head-to-head experiments, shortcuts beat menus and icons. Learning is a one-time cost; every use after that pays it back. The longer you do the same kind of work, the more it earns back.
- Benefit: A study looked at 251 experienced Word users: most rarely used the faster shortcuts and still clicked icon toolbars. Head-to-head experiments confirmed that of menus, icons and shortcuts, shortcuts are fastest. The time saved accumulates with each daily use. People doing the same kind of work long-term gain the most.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Lane, D. M., Napier, H. A., Peres, S. C., & Sándor, A. (2005). Hidden costs of graphical user interfaces: Failure to make the transition from menus and icon toolbars to keyboard shortcuts. International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction. <https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327590ijhc1802_1>
- Notes: Only learn the moves you use more than ten times a day — learning everything is another kind of waste. To be clear, the efficiency experiment had only 6 participants, and the abstract gave no seconds saved per action.

### 18. Put commute time first when choosing where to live; shorten the one-way commute

<!-- costs: money=high time=mid willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=time -->
- Cost: High. It may mean higher rent, or a smaller place. Moving itself takes time.
- In plain words: The longer the commute, the worse people feel about how life is going. The loss is not made up in pay; not in housing either. Thirty minutes less each way saves about five hours a week.
- Benefit: In May 2024, Statistics Canada put the average one-way commute at 26.4 minutes (23.9 minutes in the 2021 census). German data tracking the same people over years: the longer the commute, the steadily lower the self-reported well-being. And the loss was not compensated in income or housing. A review pulling many studies together confirmed: the longer the commute, the less satisfied people are with it, regardless of transport mode. Thirty minutes less each way saves about 5 hours a week.
- Evidence: B (disputed)
- Sources: Stutzer, A., & Frey, B. S. (2008). Stress that doesn't pay: The commuting paradox. Scandinavian Journal of Economics, 110(2), 339–366. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9442.2008.00542.x>; Chatterjee, K., et al. (2020). Commuting and wellbeing: A critical overview of the literature with implications for policy and future research. Transport Reviews, 40(1), 5–34. <https://doi.org/10.1080/01441647.2019.1649317>; Statistics Canada (2024). Average one-way commute time, May 2024. <https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/daily-quotidien/240826/t003a-eng.htm>
- Notes: Disputed. Chatterjee and colleagues, pooling many studies, found no stable link between commute length and overall life satisfaction. Their view: people largely trade the loss off with better jobs or housing. If you can shift your hours off-peak, the same route also runs shorter.

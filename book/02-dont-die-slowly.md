# 2. Don't Die Slowly

This chapter covers only two kinds of chronic risk: ones that move total mortality a lot, and ones with hard evidence. Items are ordered by cost-effectiveness. "Randomized trial" means people were randomly split into two groups — the most trustworthy kind of result. Other numbers come from studies that only watched and recorded. Those risk ratios mix in two distortions: these people were already different, and some were already sick when they adopted the habit. So those numbers show direction and rough size — not how much you personally gain by copying them.

### 1. Quit smoking, the earlier the better
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free — and it saves money. A pack in Ontario runs about $15–$20, so a pack a day is thousands a year back in your pocket. The hard part is the withdrawal weeks.
- In plain words: Smokers live ten-plus years less than never-smokers. Quitting before 40 wipes out about 90% of the extra death risk from continued smoking. Quit at 25–34 and you gain back about 10 years; at 35–44, about 9; at 45–54, about 6.
- Benefit: Jha et al. (2013), US cohort: current smokers vs never-smokers — 10+ years shorter life expectancy; quitting before 40 avoids ~90% of the excess mortality of continued smoking. Years gained: ~10 (quit 25–34), ~9 (35–44), ~6 (45–54).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Jha P et al. (2013). 21st-century hazards of smoking and benefits of cessation in the United States. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMsa1211128>
- Notes: **Ontario:** Smokers' Helpline at 1-877-513-5333 — free quit coaches. How to quit: medication (item 3), a quit date (item 4), counselling (item 5). Secondhand smoke kills too: item 2.

### 2. No smoking in the home or car — and no guests smoking indoors
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free, no time. The hard part is telling family and guests not to smoke inside.
- In plain words: About 603,000 people a year die from others' smoke, 28% of them children. Long-term secondhand exposure runs hypertension, heart disease, and stroke about 30–50% higher — and smoke breathed at home hits harder than smoke elsewhere.
- Benefit: Öberg et al. (2011): 603,000 deaths attributable to secondhand smoke in 2004 (~1.0% of global mortality); 28% in children, 61% of DALYs in children. 2026 meta-analysis (57 studies): hypertension OR 1.28, heart disease OR 1.39, myocardial infarction OR 1.50, stroke OR 1.36 (about 30–50% higher) — home exposure riskier than non-home — figures UNVERIFIED in this edition (no citable source found; see docs/verification/spot-read.md).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Öberg M et al. (2011). Worldwide burden of disease from exposure to second-hand smoke. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61388-8>
- Notes: **Ontario law:** The Smoke-Free Ontario Act s.9.2 bans smoking in a vehicle with anyone under 16 inside; smoking is also banned in enclosed workplaces and public places. Start with the home — people spend the most hours there, and children absorb 61% of secondhand smoke's health damage.

### 3. Don't quit on willpower alone — get medication first: it more than doubles success
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Nicotine patches and gum are over-the-counter, roughly $30–$60 for an 8–12 week course. Varenicline and bupropion need a prescription. The cost is roughly offset by the cigarettes you don't buy.
- In plain words: Willpower alone fails most people. Varenicline users quit at more than double the placebo rate. Nicotine replacement runs about 55% better than no medication, and patch plus a fast-acting form (gum, lozenge) adds another 27%.
- Benefit: Cochrane: varenicline vs placebo — RR 2.32 (95% CI 2.15–2.51), high-certainty; vs bupropion RR 1.36; vs single NRT RR 1.25. NRT vs control — RR 1.55 (55% better, 95% CI 1.49–1.61), 133 trials, 64,640 people. Patch + fast-acting form vs single form — RR 1.27 (27% more, 95% CI 1.17–1.37), high-certainty.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Livingstone-Banks J et al. (2023). Nicotine receptor partial agonists for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD006103.pub8>; Hartmann-Boyce J et al. (2018). Nicotine replacement therapy versus control for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD000146.pub5>
- Notes: **Canada:** NRT is OTC at any pharmacy; varenicline and bupropion need a prescription from your doctor. Varenicline commonly causes nausea and vivid dreams — tell your doctor about any psychiatric history. Pair medication with a quit date (item 4) and counselling (item 5).

### 4. Set a quit date — stop all at once, don't taper first
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=yes benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. Pick a day on the calendar and tell family and coworkers ahead of time.
- In plain words: Many plan to smoke fewer each day down to zero. At six months, 22.0% of abrupt quitters were still off cigarettes versus 15.5% of gradual reducers. So set a date and stop all at once — and start nicotine replacement two weeks before the date.
- Benefit: Lindson-Hawley et al. (2016), 697 UK smokers randomized: abrupt vs gradual — 22.0% vs 15.5% abstinent at 6 months (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.46–0.91); 49.0% vs 39.2% at 4 weeks. Starting NRT before quit day vs on the day: ~25% higher success (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.08–1.44, moderate-certainty).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lindson-Hawley N et al. (2016). Gradual Versus Abrupt Smoking Cessation. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M14-2805>
- Notes: Disputed: an older Cochrane review found the two methods similar (RR 0.94), but it was withdrawn in 2019 and is no longer updated — the randomized trial above is the best current evidence. Benefit graded mid: the endpoint is quit rates, not mortality.

### 5. Use a quit clinic or helpline — medication plus follow-up beats either alone
<!-- costs: money=low time=mid willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: The helpline is free. A clinic visit is OHIP-covered via your family doctor — typically 4–8 sessions of 30–60 minutes.
- In plain words: Pills and "someone checking in on you" are two different things, and together they work best: medication plus counselling quits at nearly double the rate of brief advice alone. Proactive quitline callbacks add about 38%.
- Benefit: Cochrane (Stead 2016; 52 trials, 19,488 people): combined pharmacotherapy + behavioural support vs usual care — RR 1.83 (95% CI 1.68–1.98), high-quality. Telephone counselling (Matkin 2019): multiple proactive callbacks vs materials only — RR 1.38 (38% more, 95% CI 1.19–1.61), moderate-certainty.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Stead LF et al. (2016). Combined pharmacotherapy and behavioural interventions for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008286.pub3>; Matkin W et al. (2019). Telephone counselling for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002850.pub4>
- Notes: **Ontario:** Smokers' Helpline at 1-877-513-5333 — free quit coaches by phone, text, and online. Ask your family doctor or pharmacist for a referral too. Most trials were outside Canada and clinic intensity varies, so read the numbers as direction. Medication: item 3.

### 6. Consider vaping only if nothing else worked — never-smokers stay away
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Device plus pods, tens of dollars a month. Buy only at licensed retailers.
- In plain words: Switching to nicotine vapes beats nicotine patches by about 61% for quitting — roughly 4 extra quitters per 100. But vaping is not harmless, and long-term safety data don't exist yet. If you don't smoke, it offers nothing.
- Benefit: Lindson et al. (2026), living review, 80 trials, 29,861 people: nicotine e-cigarettes vs NRT — RR 1.61 (61% better, 95% CI 1.23–2.12), high-certainty, ~4 more quitters per 100 (1–7). Serious adverse events: similar between groups.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lindson N et al. (2026). Electronic cigarettes for smoking cessation. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010216.pub11>
- Notes: **Canada:** The Tobacco and Vaping Products Act bans sales to minors (19+ in Ontario); provinces restrict flavours and where vapes may be sold — buy only from licensed retailers, never off social media. Disputed: WHO does not recommend vapes for quitting (long-term data gaps, youth uptake); the UK includes them in stop-smoking services. The trials used regulated nicotine products — mystery oils are a different, untested thing. Try item 3's medications first: harder evidence, cheaper.

### 7. Don't drink sugary drinks — switching to diet doesn't fix it
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free — and water or unsweetened tea saves money. The hard part is breaking the sweet-drink habit, diet versions included.
- In plain words: Two-plus sugary drinks a day runs about 21% higher death; one to two a day, about 14% higher. Diet drinks show no benefit: two-plus cups a day ran about 26% higher.
- Benefit: Malik et al. (2019), ~118,000 US adults: ≥2 servings/day HR 1.21 (21% higher), 1–2/day HR 1.14 (14% higher) vs <1/month. EPIC (450,000 people, 10 countries): sugary ≥2 glasses/day HR 1.08 (95% CI 1.01–1.16); artificially sweetened HR 1.26 (26% higher, 1.16–1.35); all soft drinks HR 1.17 (1.11–1.22).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Malik VS et al. (2019). Long-Term Consumption of Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages and Risk of Mortality in US Adults. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037401>; Mullee A et al. (2019). Association Between Soft Drink Consumption and Mortality in 10 European Countries. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.2478>
- Notes: Disputed on diet drinks: the stronger link for sweeteners is likely reverse causation — overweight and diabetic people switch to diet. Don't read it as "sweeteners are more toxic" — but "diet is fine" has no evidence behind it either. Water or unsweetened tea is the safe switch.

### 8. Don't chew betel nut
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free — and it saves the money you'd spend on it. The hard part is breaking the chewing habit.
- In plain words: Chewers run about 21% higher death, about 47% more diabetes, and about 51% more metabolic syndrome than non-chewers. Oral and oesophageal cancer sit on top of that.
- Benefit: Yamada et al. (2013), meta-analysis of 17 Asian studies, 388,000 people: all-cause mortality RR 1.21 (21% higher, P=0.02); diabetes RR 1.47 (47% higher); metabolic syndrome RR 1.51 (51% higher). Betel nut is a known cause of oral and oesophageal cancer.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Yamada T, Hara K, Kadowaki T (2013). Chewing betel quid and the risk of metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality. PLoS One. <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0070679>
- Notes: **Canada:** Betel nut isn't a regulated product here, but it's sold in some South and Southeast Asian grocery stores — the evidence travels with the habit. Longer years and more per day raise oral-cancer risk further; risk falls after quitting. Tobacco-containing quids are worse.

### 9. Swap table salt for low-sodium (potassium) salt
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: A bag costs a few dollars more. Grab it on a normal shop — no extra time, nearly no taste difference.
- In plain words: In stroke survivors and hypertensives over 60, switching to low-sodium salt meant about 12% lower death and 14% fewer strokes over five years.
- Benefit: Neal et al. (2021), randomized trial, 20,995 rural Chinese adults (prior stroke or 60+ with hypertension), 4.74 years: low-sodium salt vs regular — death RR 0.88 (12% lower), stroke RR 0.86 (14% lower), major cardiovascular events RR 0.87. No significant difference in high-potassium events.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Neal B et al. (2021). Effect of Salt Substitution on Cardiovascular Events and Death. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2105675>
- Notes: Disputed: the PURE observational study found both very low (<3 g/day urinary sodium) and very high (>7 g/day) excretion linked to higher risk — but the trial's substitution doesn't push sodium that low. The trial population was high-risk older adults; healthy young people gain far less. Kidney disease or potassium-sparing drugs: ask your doctor first.

### 10. Brush well, clean between teeth daily, replace missing teeth
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Floss or interdental brushes run about $10–$20 a year; a cleaning $100–$300. Two to three extra minutes a day. The hard part is making it daily.
- In plain words: Daily interdental cleaning runs about 11% lower death. Older adults with no teeth run about 87% higher death; under 20 teeth, about double.
- Benefit: Wang et al. (2026), 9,676 Japanese adults, 6 years: interdental cleaning HR 0.89 (11% lower). Ko et al. (2026), meta-analysis of community-dwelling older adults: edentulous vs dentate OR 1.87 (87% higher, 95% CI 1.35–2.59); <20 teeth OR 2.04 (1.67–2.49).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Wang K et al. (2026). Routine oral health practices and all-cause mortality. Journal of Dentistry. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdent.2026.106789>; Ko MJ et al. (2026). Deteriorated oral health and function as risk factors for physical disability and mortality in community-dwelling older adults. European Geriatric Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1007/s41999-025-01319-4>
- Notes: **Canada:** Dental care is mostly private, but the Canadian Dental Care Plan covers eligible residents — check eligibility. Disputed: these are follow-up studies — people with bad teeth are often sicker and poorer overall, so tooth vs body causation is unclear. Either way the cost is trivial, and gum disease and missing teeth already hurt eating.

### 11. Walk 7,000–8,000 steps a day
<!-- costs: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. Sixty to ninety minutes a day, splittable into commutes and errands. The hard part is doing it daily.
- In plain words: People walking 5,800 steps a day run about 40% lower death than those at 3,500; at 7,800 steps, about 45% lower. Past a point the curve flattens — 6,000–8,000 is enough at 60+.
- Benefit: Paluch et al. (2022), meta-analysis of 15 cohorts, 47,471 people, 3,013 deaths: vs lowest quartile (median 3,553 steps/day, about 3,500) — 5,801 steps (about 5,800) HR 0.60 (40% lower), 7,842 steps (about 7,800) HR 0.55 (45% lower), 10,901 steps HR 0.47. Plateau at 6,000–8,000 steps for 60+, 8,000–10,000 under 60.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Paluch AE et al. (2022). Daily steps and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis of 15 international cohorts. Lancet Public Health. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S2468-2667(21)00302-9>
- Notes: The 4,000→7,000 stretch is the best value — no need to force 10,000. These are follow-up studies: the least-walking group includes people already too sick to walk, so the gaps read too large — but the more-is-better trend is clear. Same thing as item 14 in different units; hit one of them.

### 12. High blood pressure or cholesterol: take the pills on schedule, don't stop on your own
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Generic blood-pressure pills and statins cost a few dollars a month with coverage. Once a day, seconds. The hard part is never stopping.
- In plain words: Every 10 mmHg lower blood pressure means about 13% lower death and 27% fewer strokes. Statins: each 1.0 mmol/L lower LDL means about 10% lower death. People taking ≥80% of doses run 29–45% lower death than frequent skippers.
- Benefit: Randomized-trial meta-analyses: per 10 mmHg lower systolic — death RR 0.87 (13% lower), major cardiovascular events RR 0.80, stroke RR 0.73 (27% fewer), heart failure RR 0.72 (Ettehad 2016). Per 1.0 mmol/L lower LDL — death RR 0.90 (10% lower), major vascular events RR 0.78 (CTT 2010). Adherence meta-analysis: ≥80% adherent vs not — statins RR 0.55 (45% lower), antihypertensives RR 0.71 (29% lower) (Chowdhury 2013).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Ettehad D et al. (2016). Blood pressure lowering for prevention of cardiovascular disease and death. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(15)01225-8>; Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' Collaboration (2010). Efficacy and safety of more intensive lowering of LDL cholesterol. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(10)61350-5>
- Notes: Only if your doctor says you need them — healthy people don't take these. The adherence numbers are observational (adherent people are more disciplined generally, inflating the benefit); the trial numbers above are the reliable ones. No separate blood-sugar item here: tighter glucose control doesn't cut total mortality as consistently as blood pressure and lipids do.

### 13. Sleep about 7 hours a night, on a fixed schedule
<!-- costs: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. You pay in time — mostly traded from phone-scrolling. The hard part is fixing bedtime and wake time.
- In plain words: Habitual short sleepers run about 12% higher death. But regularity matters more than duration: the most regular sleepers run 20–48% lower death than the most irregular. Weekend-vs-weekday sleep midpoints differing by 2+ hours mean about 30% more heart disease.
- Benefit: Meta-analyses: short sleep RR 1.12 (12% higher), long sleep RR 1.30 (Cappuccio 2010); per hour under 7, RR 1.06; per hour over 7, RR 1.13 (Yin 2017). UK Biobank wristwatch data (~61,000 people): the four more-regular groups ran 20%–48% lower death than the most irregular (Windred 2024). Social jet lag ≥2 h (2+ hours): HR 1.30 (30% more, 95% CI 1.11–1.54) for cardiovascular disease (Kumar 2026).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Cappuccio FP et al. (2010). Sleep duration and all-cause mortality. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/33.5.585>; Windred DP et al. (2024). Sleep regularity is a stronger predictor of mortality risk than sleep duration. Sleep. <https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsad253>
- Notes: Fix short sleep and irregular schedules — don't compress sleep on purpose. Long sleepers' higher risk is mostly reverse causation (depression, chronic disease, sleep apnea make people sleep more). Regularity's evidence is two follow-up studies (B-grade alone). Catching up after a bad night: item 39.

### 14. 150–300 minutes a week of moderate exercise — brisk walking counts
<!-- costs: money=0 time=high willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. Twenty to forty-five minutes a day. The hard part is years of it.
- In plain words: People hitting 150–300 brisk-walking minutes a week run about 31% lower death than non-exercisers. Even below the guideline: about 20% lower. Three to five times the guideline maxes out around 39% — more adds nothing further, but no harm either.
- Benefit: Arem et al. (2015), pooled dose-response: 1–2× guideline (7.5–15 MET-h/week ≈ 150–300 brisk minutes) HR 0.69 (31% lower); below guideline HR 0.80 (20% lower); 3–5× HR 0.61 (39% lower, the floor); 10×+ HR 0.69. Accelerometer meta-analysis (Ekelund 2019): most vs least active quarter HR 0.52.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Arem H et al. (2015). Leisure time physical activity and mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2015.0533>; Ekelund U et al. (2019). Dose-response associations between accelerometry measured physical activity and sedentary time and all cause mortality. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.l4570>
- Notes: Pick this or item 11 — they measure the same thing. These are follow-up studies; the accelerometer ones tracked older people briefly, mixing in "already sick, so inactive." The true benefit is smaller than HR 0.52 suggests.

### 15. Racket sports three times a week, 45 minutes each
<!-- costs: money=low time=mid willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Court fees roughly $10–$30 a session. About 2 hours a week. The hard part is rounding up people and courts, weekly.
- In plain words: Regular tennis, badminton, table-tennis players run about 47% lower death and 56% lower cardiovascular death than non-players. Swimmers run about 28% lower. Running and soccer showed no difference in this dataset.
- Benefit: Oja et al. (2017), 80,306 UK adults: racket sports vs none — all-cause HR 0.53 (47% lower, 95% CI 0.40–0.69), cardiovascular death HR 0.44 (56% lower, 0.24–0.83). Swimming 0.72/0.59 (28% lower); aerobics 0.73/0.64; cycling 0.85. Running and football: no significant difference.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Oja P et al. (2017). Associations of specific types of sports and exercise with all-cause and cardiovascular-disease mortality. British Journal of Sports Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2016-096822>
- Notes: Disputed: follow-up data only — racket players were healthier and more social to begin with, and runners showing nothing suggests the picker matters more than the sport. Don't conclude running is useless; total-volume items are 11 and 14.

### 16. Stack stray bursts of effort — stairs, hurrying — to 4–5 minutes a day
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free, no extra workout time. The hard part is remembering: take the stairs, walk fast, skip the shortcut.
- In plain words: People who never exercise but get three one-to-two-minute bursts of vigorous daily activity run about 40% lower death and nearly half the cardiovascular death of people with none. Vigorous means stairs, brisk hurrying, carrying loads uphill.
- Benefit: Stamatakis et al. (2022), UK Biobank, 25,241 non-exercisers, 6.9 years, 852 deaths: any vigorous intermittent lifestyle activity vs none — all-cause and cancer death 38%–40% lower, cardiovascular death 48%–49% lower. 4.4 min/day total: 26%–30% lower all-cause, 32%–34% lower cardiovascular.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Stamatakis E et al. (2022). Association of wearable device-measured vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity with mortality. Nature Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x>
- Notes: Disputed: wearables beat questionnaires, but it's still follow-up, not a trial, with 6.9 years of tracking. Only applies to non-exercisers — regular trainers don't get this item.

### 17. 30–60 minutes a week of strength training
<!-- costs: money=0 time=mid willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Bodyweight squats and push-ups are free. One to two sessions a week, 20–30 minutes each. The hard part is keeping it on the calendar.
- In plain words: People doing 30–60 weekly minutes of strength work run 10–20% lower death than non-lifters. More is not better. Strength plus aerobic beats either alone.
- Benefit: Momma et al. (2022), meta-analysis of cohorts: strength training vs none — 10%–17% lower mortality; the biggest drop (10%–20%) at ~30–60 min/week, no extra benefit beyond. Strength + aerobic better than neither.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Momma H et al. (2022). Muscle-strengthening activities are associated with lower risk and mortality in major non-communicable diseases. British Journal of Sports Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061>
- Notes: Follow-up studies with self-reported exercise; the "too much gets worse" tail is weakly evidenced — don't cap training over it. For older adults, strength also prevents falls and preserves muscle (Chapter 1, item 13).

### 18. Don't sit for hours unbroken — get up and move periodically
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free; standing up takes seconds. The hard part is remembering once you're sunk in — set a reminder.
- In plain words: The most sedentary people run 2.6 times the death of the least sedentary; the longest unbroken sitters run about double the shortest. But 60–75 daily minutes of moderate activity essentially erases the excess.
- Benefit: Diaz et al. (2017), US cohort with accelerometers: most vs least total sedentary time HR 2.63 (about 2.6×); longest vs shortest uninterrupted bouts HR 1.96. Ekelund et al. (2016), 1M+ people: 8+ h/day sitting with almost no activity HR 1.59 vs <4 h + most active; with 60–75 min/day moderate activity, even 8+ h sitters showed HR 1.04 — no significant difference.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Diaz KM et al. (2017). Patterns of Sedentary Behavior and Mortality in U.S. Middle-Aged and Older Adults. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M17-0212>; Ekelund U et al. (2016). Does physical activity attenuate, or even eliminate, the detrimental association of sitting time with mortality? Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)30370-1>
- Notes: The reliable takeaway: how much you move matters more than how long you sit. The 2.63× includes plenty of already-sick sitters, so it reads too high. Same review: 3+ h/day of TV kept its risk at every activity level (the most active only pushed the threshold to 5 h).

### 19. Eat less processed meat (ham, bacon, sausage, luncheon meat)
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free — it saves money, no time. The hard part is eating less of something you like.
- In plain words: The heaviest processed-meat eaters run about 23% higher death than the lightest. Each extra daily serving runs about 23% higher too.
- Benefit: Larsson & Orsini (2014), meta-analysis: highest vs lowest processed meat — RR 1.23 (23% higher); red meat total RR 1.29; unprocessed red meat RR 1.10 (not significant). Schwingshackl et al. (2017): per daily serving, processed meat RR 1.23, red meat RR 1.10.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Larsson SC, Orsini N (2014). Red meat and processed meat consumption and all-cause mortality: a meta-analysis. American Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwt261>; Johnston BC et al. (2019). Unprocessed Red Meat and Processed Meat Consumption: Dietary Guideline Recommendations From the NutriRECS Consortium. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M19-1621>
- Notes: Disputed: NutriRECS graded these studies low-certainty (GRADE) and gave only a weak "keep eating as now" recommendation. The fight is over how solid the evidence is, not its direction — no study finds processed meat beneficial. Unprocessed red meat's effect is small and not significant. **Canada:** Canada's Food Guide advises limiting processed meats.

### 20. Drink little or no alcohol
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free — it saves money, no time. The hard part is the awkwardness of declining at social dinners.
- In plain words: Past 100 g of pure alcohol a week (about 2.5 litres of 5% beer), the more you drink the earlier you die. At 40: 100–200 g/week costs about 6 months; 200–350 g costs 1–2 years; over 350 g costs 4–5 years. For total health, the best amount is zero.
- Benefit: Wood et al. (2018), 83 cohorts, 599,912 drinkers: lowest mortality at ≤100 g/week (~2.5 L of 5% beer or 300 mL of 40% spirits). Life lost at 40: ~6 months (100–200 g/week), 1–2 years (200–350 g), 4–5 years (>350 g). GBD 2016: the level minimizing total health loss is 0 drinks/week.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Wood AM et al. (2018). Risk thresholds for alcohol consumption. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)30134-X>; GBD 2016 Alcohol Collaborators (2018). Alcohol use and burden for 195 countries and territories. Lancet. <https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31310-2>
- Notes: **Canada:** The Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction (CCSA) guidance: no more than 2 standard drinks a week to keep risk low. Disputed: one 34-study pooling found up to 17–18% lower mortality at small amounts (men ≤4 drinks/day, women ≤2) — but its non-drinker group mixed in ex-drinkers and the already-ill; a bias-corrected pooling erased the protection. "Start drinking for your health" has no basis. Cutting back: item 22. Daily drinkers must not quit cold turkey: item 21.

### 21. Daily heavy drinker with shakes when you stop: don't quit cold turkey
<!-- costs: money=low time=mid willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: A doctor or hospital visit — covered by OHIP. Severe cases need a few days admitted.
- In plain words: Long-term heavy drinkers who stop suddenly risk withdrawal: tremor, sweating, racing heart, insomnia at the mild end — seizures, delirium tremens at the severe end, and that is a fatal emergency. In Norway's registry, 8.0% of the delirium-tremens group died each year. Tell your doctor exactly how much you drink daily.
- Benefit: Caputo et al. (2026) clinical review: alcohol withdrawal syndrome after abrupt stop or sharp cut — tremor, autonomic hyperactivity, anxiety, insomnia, perceptual disturbances, seizures, delirium tremens — "common and potentially life-threatening" in general medical care. Bramness et al. (2023), 36,287 Norwegians: annual mortality 8.0% (delirium tremens), 5.0% (withdrawal state), 3.6% (dependence); delirium-tremens SMR 9.8 (95% CI 8.9–10.7).
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Caputo F et al. (2026). Alcohol withdrawal syndrome in hospitalized patients: a practical review. European Journal of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejim.2026.107103>; Bramness JG et al. (2023). Mortality and alcohol-related morbidity in patients with delirium tremens, alcohol withdrawal state or alcohol dependence in Norway. Addiction. <https://doi.org/10.1111/add.16297>
- Notes: **Ontario:** Go to the emergency department or your doctor — withdrawal is managed with medication plus vitamin B1 (thiamine). How to tell: drinking nearly daily, shaking/sweating/racing heart without a drink, needing a morning drink — any one of these means don't stop alone. Support: ConnexOntario at 1-866-531-2600. Graded B because the cohort describes people who already had delirium tremens, not people quitting cold turkey — the link is inference. You still quit — just in hospital. How much is too much: item 20. Cutting back: item 22.

### 22. To drink less: count your weekly drinks first, then talk to your doctor for a few minutes
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. Log each drink daily; a few minutes with your doctor costs nothing extra.
- In plain words: A few minutes of advice — reviewing your intake, laying out the risks, setting a target — meant about 20 g less pure alcohol a week a year later, roughly half a litre of beer. Longer sessions added nothing. Two medications help people who can't stop: about one extra person in 12 stays off heavy drinking.
- Benefit: Kaner et al. (2018), 34 randomized trials, 15,197 people: brief intervention vs minimal/no intervention — 20 g/week less alcohol at one year (95% CI 12–28 g), moderate-quality; longer counselling added no benefit. Jonas et al. (2014), 122 trials, 22,803 people: acamprosate NNT 12 (95% CI 8–26) for return to any drinking; oral naltrexone 50 mg/day NNT 12 (8–26) for return to heavy drinking.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Kaner EF et al. (2018). Effectiveness of brief alcohol interventions in primary care populations. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. <https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD004148.pub4>; Jonas DE et al. (2014). Pharmacotherapy for adults with alcohol use disorders in outpatient settings. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2014.3628>
- Notes: Benefit graded small: the trials measured drinks, not deaths — a surrogate endpoint by this book's rules. The drug trials were mostly in already-dependent patients who also got counselling; the numbers are the extra benefit on top of that. Whether these drugs are indicated and available to you is your doctor's call — don't order them online. Already in withdrawal: item 21.

### 23. A small handful of nuts a day
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: 28 g a day, roughly $200–$300 a year. Eat them as-is, no time.
- In plain words: Regular nut eaters run about 20% lower death than non-eaters. Once a week already shows about 11% lower; a daily handful shows the most.
- Benefit: Bao et al. (2013), two US cohorts, 119,000 people: vs never — <1/week HR 0.93 (95% CI 0.90–0.96), 1/week 0.89 (11% lower), 2–4/week 0.87, 5–6/week 0.85, 7+/week 0.80 (0.73–0.86, 20% lower).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Bao Y, Han J, Hu FB, et al. (2013). Association of nut consumption with total and cause-specific mortality. New England Journal of Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1307352>
- Notes: Buy plain, unsalted. Nuts are calorie-dense — don't graze freely. Follow-up data only: nut eaters live healthier overall, so discount the numbers.

### 24. Swap some red meat for fish and poultry
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=low dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free, no time. This swaps one meat for another — not extra spending. The hard part is a small taste adjustment.
- In plain words: Two extra servings a week of processed and red meat runs about 3% higher death; the same servings as fish, poultry show no increase. This effect is small — a no-effort swap, not something to agonize over.
- Benefit: Zhong et al. (2020), 6 US cohorts pooled, ~30,000 people: per 2 extra servings/week — processed meat HR 1.03 (3% higher, 95% CI 1.02–1.05), unprocessed red meat HR 1.03 (3% higher, 1.01–1.05); poultry HR 0.99 (0.97–1.02), fish HR 0.99 (0.97–1.01) — neither significant.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Zhong VW et al. (2020). Associations of Processed Meat, Unprocessed Red Meat, Poultry, or Fish Intake With Incident Cardiovascular Disease and All-Cause Mortality. JAMA Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.6969>
- Notes: Two servings a week moves the needle very little — don't expect extra years from the swap. Same money, pick fish and poultry first.

### 25. Swap some white rice and refined flour for whole grains
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Brown rice, oats, and whole-wheat flour cost a little more; no extra time. The hard part is adjusting to the texture.
- In plain words: People eating 90 g more whole grains a day run about 17% lower death — 90 g is about three servings. The benefit keeps growing to about 210–225 g a day.
- Benefit: Aune et al. (2016), dose-response meta-analysis: per 90 g/day more whole grains — RR 0.83 (17% lower); benefit continues to 210–225 g/day. Schwingshackl et al. (2017): per serving RR 0.92.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2016). Whole grain consumption and risk of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and all cause and cause specific mortality. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.i2716>
- Notes: No need to switch everything — half swapped already lands in the best-value stretch. Follow-up data only: whole-grain eaters were healthier to begin with, inflating the numbers, and studies disagree widely (I² 83%). **Canada:** Canada's Food Guide — make a quarter of your plate whole grains. Benefit graded mid (17% < 20% threshold), not high.

### 26. Tea three or more times a week
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Tens of dollars a year. Minutes to brew.
- In plain words: Habitual tea drinkers run about 15% lower death. From age 50, they average 1.26 extra years of life and 1.41 extra years free of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
- Benefit: Wang et al. (2020), China-PAR, 100,900 people: habitual tea drinking — HR 0.85 (15% lower, 95% CI 0.79–0.90); from age 50: +1.26 years life expectancy, +1.41 years free of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Wang X, Liu F, Li J, et al. (2020). Tea consumption and the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality: The China-PAR project. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1177/2047487319894685>
- Notes: Disputed: follow-up data only, and Chinese male tea drinkers smoke and drink more — partly adjusted, never fully. Don't drink it scalding: item 31. Mouldy tea: store dry, discard damp tea. Green-tea extract capsules are different — concentrated catechins in one gulp can injure the liver; brewed tea doesn't do that. This item means brewed tea only.

### 27. Three to four cups of coffee a day, black with no sugar
<!-- costs: money=low time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Home-brewed, $1–$2 a day. Minutes per cup.
- In plain words: People drinking three to four cups a day run about 17% lower death than non-drinkers. Black — sugar and cream caps cancel the benefit.
- Benefit: Poole et al. (2017), umbrella review of 201 meta-analyses: 3–4 cups/day vs none — RR 0.83 (17% lower, 95% CI 0.79–0.88).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Poole R, Kennedy OJ, Roderick P, et al. (2017). Coffee consumption and health: umbrella review of meta-analyses of multiple health outcomes. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.j5024>
- Notes: Disputed: the authors state the evidence is almost entirely observational and call for proper randomized trials before claiming causation. Pregnancy, arrhythmia, anxiety, insomnia — skip this one. Benefit graded mid (17% < 20% threshold), not high.

### 28. Five servings (about 400 g) of fruit and vegetables a day
<!-- costs: money=low time=mid willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: A few dollars a day. Daily washing and chopping takes time. The hard part is buying and eating enough, every day.
- In plain words: Each extra 200 g a day runs about 10% lower death. Five servings a day runs about 13% lower than two. Two fruit plus three vegetables is the sweet spot — more adds nothing.
- Benefit: Aune et al. (2017), dose-response meta-analysis: per 200 g/day — RR 0.90 (10% lower); benefit to 800 g/day. Wang et al. (2021), 2 US cohorts + 26-study meta-analysis: 5 vs 2 servings/day — HR 0.87 (13% lower); best at 2 fruit + 3 vegetables, no further drop above.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Aune D et al. (2017). Fruit and vegetable intake and the risk of cardiovascular disease, total cancer and all-cause mortality. International Journal of Epidemiology. <https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw319>; Wang DD et al. (2021). Fruit and Vegetable Intake and Mortality. Circulation. <https://doi.org/10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996>
- Notes: Five is enough — both studies agree ten buys nothing extra. Follow-up data only: big-vegetable eaters differ in income, education, and exercise too, so "10% lower" is already an upper bound. **Canada:** Canada's Food Guide — half your plate vegetables and fruit.

### 29. Eat less ultra-processed food (chips, instant noodles, pastries, ready meals)
<!-- costs: money=0 time=high willpower=yes benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free to swap; cooking from less-processed ingredients takes daily time. The hard part is years of resisting convenience.
- In plain words: The heaviest ultra-processed eaters run about 21% higher death and 50% higher cardiovascular death than the lightest. But this evidence is graded low-certainty — read the numbers discounted.
- Benefit: Lane et al. (2024), umbrella review: high vs low ultra-processed intake — all-cause RR 1.21 (21% higher), cardiovascular death RR 1.50 (50% higher) ("highly suggestive" and "convincing" evidence grades); GRADE certainty low to very low.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lane MM et al. (2024). Ultra-processed food exposure and adverse health outcomes: umbrella review of epidemiological meta-analyses. BMJ. <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077310>
- Notes: Disputed on three counts: the NOVA classification lumps nutritionally different foods together; it overlaps heavily with sugary drinks and processed meat (items 7 and 19), so the independent effect is unclear; and GRADE rates the certainty low. Do items 7 and 19 first, then consider this one.

### 30. Don't cook or heat with coal and wood — switch to electric or gas
<!-- costs: money=high time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: A stove upgrade plus fuel runs hundreds to thousands of dollars — a one-time cost, no ongoing time. (City households are already on electric or gas.)
- In plain words: Cooking with coal and wood runs about 11% higher death; heating with it about 14% higher. Households that switched to clean fuels ran 13–33% lower than those still burning.
- Benefit: Yu et al. (2018), 271,000 Chinese adults without cardiovascular disease, follow-up: solid-fuel cooking vs clean — HR 1.11 (11% higher); solid-fuel heating — HR 1.14 (14% higher). Switched to clean fuels vs still burning: cooking HR 0.87 (13% lower), heating HR 0.67 (33% lower).
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Yu K et al. (2018). Association of Solid Fuel Use With Risk of Cardiovascular and All-Cause Mortality in Rural China. JAMA. <https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2018.2151>
- Notes: **Canada:** Replace old uncertified wood stoves with EPA/CSA-certified models or heat pumps; burn only dry, seasoned wood; never run generators or barbecues indoors (carbon monoxide). Follow-up data only: switchers were also wealthier, so the 0.67 figure mixes in affluence. Outdoor PM2.5 is largely beyond personal control — moving, masks, and purifiers are the options, and purifiers have no mortality trials.

### 31. Don't drink, eat, or bathe in water hotter than about 65°C (maté, very hot tea, porridge, hot springs)
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. A little patience to let food cool.
- In plain words: People drinking "very hot" maté run 4.1 times the oesophageal cancer of non-drinkers; hot (not very hot) is about double. Very hot drinks — hot tea and others — run about 90% higher oesophageal cancer. The "65°C" line comes from IARC's official classification: drinking anything above 65°C is "probably carcinogenic to humans".
- Benefit: Lubin et al. (2014), case-control: very hot maté OR 4.14 (about 4.1×, 95% CI 3.25–5.29); hot 1.81 (1.42–2.31); warm 1.00 — figures UNVERIFIED in this edition (cited DOI does not resolve; see docs/verification/link-sweep.md). Islami et al. (2009), systematic review: hot beverages — oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma RR 1.91 (about 90% higher, 95% CI 1.64–2.23). IARC Monographs: "very hot beverages above 65°C" — Group 2A, probably carcinogenic.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Lubin JH et al. (2014). Maté intake and risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma in Uruguay. Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers & Prevention. DOI 10.1158/1055-9965.EPI-13-0346 — UNVERIFIED (does not resolve as of 2026-10-01); IARC Monographs Working Group (2016). Very hot beverages at or above 65°C are a probable cause of cancer of the oesophagus. <https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr244_E.pdf>
- Notes: Group 2A is probability, not a measured effect size — so Benefit carries no exact number. Case-control evidence is graded B here (recall bias); IARC's official classification pulls the item grade to A. "Hot" vs "very hot" came from each participant's own description — no thermometer in the studies. Practical rule: if it burns your lip, wait; a thermometer costs under $20. **Ontario:** Hot tubs and saunas — follow the facility's posted limits. Bathing water this hot can also burn skin — keep hot water tanks at or below 60°C against scalds, and never pour near-boiling water on skin.

### 32. When brushing and flossing, also brush your tongue
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. A few seconds of tongue brushing on top of what you already do.
- In plain words: People who brush their teeth three-plus times a day run about 14% lower heart failure; tongue cleaning may help too, though this evidence is graded low — read the numbers discounted. Tongue brushing cuts the bacteria that give bad breath by 53–75% in the best studies.
- Benefit: Chang et al. (2020), 161,286 Koreans: tooth brushing ≥3/day vs 0–1 — HR 0.86 (14% lower, 95% CI 0.82–0.90) for heart failure; also fewer cardiovascular events and deaths. Pedrazzi et al. (2004), randomized: tongue brushing cut volatile sulphur compounds 53–75%.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Chang Y et al. (2020). Improved oral hygiene care is associated with decreased risk of new-onset atrial fibrillation and heart failure. European Journal of Preventive Cardiology. <https://doi.org/10.1177/2047487319886018>; Pedrazzi V et al. (2004). Tongue-cleaning methods: a comparative clinical trial employing a toothbrush and a tongue scraper. Special Care in Dentistry. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1754-4505.2004.tb01694.x>
- Notes: Disputed: the heart-failure finding is observational — frequent brushers differ in every health behaviour. The breath trial was randomized but tiny (10 people) and short. This item's practical payload is oral health and breath, not years of life — treat the 14% as suggestive, not a promise. Benefit graded mid (14% < 20%).

### 33. Take a full two-day weekend every week, with no overtime
<!-- costs: money=0 time=high willpower=yes benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free in money; costs you two full days a week. The hard part is turning down overtime and career pressure.
- In plain words: The WHO and ILO attribute 745,000 deaths a year worldwide to working 55-plus hours a week. In China's national data, people with a two-day weekend and no overtime ran 41% lower death than people with no days off and overtime. The gap shrank after adjusting for everything else, so discount it.
- Benefit: Pega et al. (2021), WHO/ILO: working ≥55 h/week vs 35–40 h — stroke RR 1.35 (95% CI 1.13–1.61), ischaemic heart disease RR 1.17 (1.05–1.31); 745,194 deaths (about 745,000) attributable to long hours in 2016. Wang (2025), 29,000 Chinese workers: two-day weekend without overtime vs no rest with overtime — mortality HR 0.59 (41% lower, 95% CI 0.36–0.97); adjusted HR 0.71 (0.44–1.15), no longer significant.
- Evidence: B
- Sources: Pega F et al. (2021). Global, regional, and national burdens of ischemic heart disease and stroke attributable to exposure to long working hours. Environment International. <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envint.2021.106595>; Wang P et al. (2025). Association between rest days and all-cause mortality in China: a nationally representative cohort. PMC. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12311587/>
- Notes: Disputed: the Chinese "41% lower" fades to nothing after full adjustment — the strongest claim is only about the ≥55-hour threshold (15% more stroke). If overtime is forced on you, that's the next chapter's domain — overwork protection is in 06 (labour) and the money chapters. Graded B: the rest-day numbers are single-cohort and adjusted-away.

### 34. Don't sit or lie down all day — get up and move every half hour
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=high dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. No extra time — just standing up more often.
- In plain words: People sitting 13.2+ hours a day ran about 2.63 times the death of people sitting under 6.5 hours — and this is after adjusting for exercise, so moving half an hour in the evening doesn't cancel a sedentary day. Regular movement breaks cut the blood-sugar and blood-pressure spikes that sitting causes.
- Benefit: Diaz et al. (2017), US cohort: sedentary ≥13.2 h/day vs <6.5 h — HR 2.63 (95% CI 1.60–4.30), adjusted for physical activity. Ekelund et al. (2016): high activity attenuated but did not eliminate the sedentary risk.
- Evidence: A
- Sources: Diaz KM et al. (2017). Patterns of Sedentary Behavior and Mortality in U.S. Middle-Aged and Older Adults. Annals of Internal Medicine. <https://doi.org/10.7326/M17-0212>; Ekelund U et al. - Notes: Standing all day has its own problems (veins, back) — the point is alternating, not standing still. If you stand for work, sit-breaks count too.

### 36. Make sure you get some direct sunlight every day (without burning)
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=no benefit=low dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. Minutes — the time you'd spend outside anyway.
- In plain words: People avoiding the sun run about double the death of the most sun-exposed group — the link runs through vitamin D and physical activity, so read it discounted. Ten to fifteen minutes a day in summer is enough for most people.
- Benefit: Lindqvist et al. (2014), 29,518 Swedish women, 20 years: sun avoiders vs most active sun seekers — HR 2.0 (95% CI 1.6–2.5); non-smoking sun avoiders had similar life expectancy to smokers with the most sun.

### 39. 25 and up: cervical cancer screening every five years

### 40. Organ donation: register as a donor, and tell your family

### 41. A living will: write down your end-of-life wishes while you're healthy
<!-- costs: money=0 time=low willpower=some benefit=mid dimension=death -->
- Cost: Free. An hour of thinking and writing, plus the conversation with family.
- In plain words: Writing down what treatments you want — and don't want — when you can no longer speak means your wishes get followed instead of guessed. It also spares your family from making those decisions blind.
- Benefit: No mortality trial exists or could exist — the benefit is autonomy: wishes documented in advance are followed far more often than wishes never written down, and families report less distress and conflict when a directive exists.
- Evidence: C
- Sources: Ontario, Health Care Consent Act, 1996, S.O. 1996, c. 2, Sched. A (substitute decision-making and advance care planning framework). <https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/96h02>
- Notes: **Ontario:** Called advance care planning — name a substitute decision-maker and write your wishes; hospitals and long-term care homes must follow the Health Care Consent Act hierarchy.
